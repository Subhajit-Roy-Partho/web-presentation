Background — Biology

Proteins: The Molecules of Life

Proteins are linear polymers of amino acids that fold into precise 3-D structures governing virtually every cellular function.

  • Primary structure: sequence of 20 amino acid types linked by peptide bonds; encoded by DNA
  • Secondary structure: local H-bond patterns form α-helices and β-sheets
  • Tertiary structure: global 3-D fold stabilised by hydrophobic core, disulfide bridges, electrostatics
  • Backbone representation: each residue described by N–Cα–C atoms and dihedral angles φ, ψ, ω
Key insight: Knowing the sequence does not tell you the dynamics — a single sequence populates an ensemble of conformations.
Slide 3 of 22
Background — Biology

Proteins Are Not Static

At physiological temperature, proteins constantly fluctuate among many conformations. This conformational ensemble is governed by thermodynamics.

  • Free energy landscape: multiple minima separated by barriers; each basin = a distinct functional state
  • Boltzmann distribution: population of state i ∝ exp(−Gi/kBT)
  • RMSF (Root-Mean-Square Fluctuation): quantifies per-residue flexibility — the experimental ground truth
\( p(\mathbf{x}) \propto e^{-U(\mathbf{x})/k_BT} \)

Function — enzymatic catalysis, signal transduction, allostery — often depends on which conformation and how fast transitions occur.

Background — Simulation

Molecular Dynamics Simulation

MD numerically integrates Newton's equations for all atoms using a parameterised force field.

\( m_i\ddot{\mathbf{r}}_i = -\nabla_{\mathbf{r}_i} U(\mathbf{r}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{r}_N) \)
  • Force field (e.g. AMBER ff14SB): bonds, angles, torsions, LJ, electrostatics
  • Time step: 2 fs — limited by fastest bond vibrations
  • NPT ensemble: constant pressure and temperature via barostat/thermostat
  • ATLAS dataset: 1,400 proteins × 300 ns = 420 μs of trajectories
Cost: A single 300 ns trajectory requires ~108 integration steps and weeks of GPU time.

The Problem

The Timescale Gap

MD is limited to nanosecond–microsecond timescales, while biologically relevant events occur on milliseconds to seconds.

MD Time Step

2 fs — set by fastest bond vibrations (C–H stretch)

Biological Timescale

Protein folding, allostery, and catalysis occur on μs–ms

The Gap

106–109 steps needed; months of GPU time for one trajectory

The Problem — Context

Existing Approaches Fall Short

Each prior method solves part of the problem but fails on temporal coherence or scalability.

Normal Mode Analysis

Idea: Harmonic approximation around single minimum; eigenvectors capture motions.

Limit: Linear model; misses large-amplitude, anharmonic motions. RMSF r ≈ 0.64 vs. MDGen r ≈ 0.87.

Boltzmann Generators

Idea: Normalizing flow learns the equilibrium distribution p(x).

Limit: Generates independent i.i.d. samples — no temporal ordering, no kinetics information.

Independent Frame Diffusion

Idea: FrameDiff, AlphaFlow, BioEmu generate one frame at a time via SE(3) diffusion.

Limit: Each frame is independent — autocorrelation function and frame-to-frame step sizes are wrong.

AlphaFold2 / ESMFold

Idea: Sequence → single most-probable structure.

Limit: Predicts one static conformation; no dynamics, no ensemble.

Enhanced Sampling MD

Idea: Replica exchange, metadynamics push the system over barriers.

Limit: Still atomistic — expensive; distorts the kinetics (non-physical biasing forces).

MDGen (Proposed)

First method to jointly achieve equilibrium correctness and temporal coherence at generative-model speed.

r ≈ 0.87 · JSD ≈ 0.08 · ~10,000× faster than MD

ML Background

Diffusion Models 101

Diffusion models learn to reverse a noise-injection process. They are the backbone of state-of-the-art generative AI (Stable Diffusion, AlphaFold3, etc.).

Forward (noising): \( q(\mathbf{x}_t|\mathbf{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0,\,(1-\bar\alpha_t)\mathbf{I}) \)
Reverse (denoising) — learned: \( p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_{t-1}|\mathbf{x}_t) = \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t,t),\,\sigma_t^2\mathbf{I}) \)
  • Score function s(x,t) = ∇log pt(x) tells the network which direction reduces noise
  • Training: minimize MSE between predicted and true noise (denoising score matching)
  • Sampling: start from Gaussian noise, iteratively denoise for 500 (DDPM) or 50 (DDIM) steps

ML Background

SE(3) Equivariance & Backbone Frames

Protein physics is invariant to global rotation and translation. Our model must respect this symmetry.

  • SE(3) = Special Euclidean group in 3-D: rotations SO(3) ⋊ translations ℝ³
  • Equivariance: if we rotate the input protein, the output rotates consistently
  • Backbone frame: each residue i carries a local coordinate system (Ri, ti) built from N–Cα–C atoms
\( T_i = (R_i,\,\mathbf{t}_i) \in \text{SE}(3), \quad R_i \in \text{SO}(3), \; \mathbf{t}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3 \)

MDGen represents a trajectory of L residues over F frames as a tensor of SE(3) elements: T ∈ SE(3)F×L.

Why it matters: Without equivariance the model must wastefully learn the same physics for every possible orientation of the protein.
Slide 9 of 22
ML Background

Diffusion on Non-Euclidean Spaces

Protein backbone degrees of freedom live on curved manifolds, not flat Euclidean space. Standard Gaussian diffusion must be adapted.

Rotations — SO(3)

Rotation matrices R ∈ SO(3) form a curved 3-D manifold. Naive Gaussian diffusion would leave the manifold (det ≠ 1).

\( q(R_t | R_0) = \text{IGSO}(3)(\sigma_t) \)

Isotropic Gaussian on SO(3) — closed-form score via heat kernel expansion. Handles arbitrary 3-D rotations of each backbone frame.

Torsions — Flat Torus 𝕋²

Dihedral angles φ, ψ are periodic in [−π, π). Standard Gaussian wraps around the period.

\( q(\theta_t|\theta_0) = \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} \mathcal{N}(\theta_0 + 2\pi k,\,\sigma_t^2) \)

Wrapped Gaussian kernel on the torus — correct periodicity for Ramachandran dihedral angles.

MDGen's forward process applies IGSO(3) noise to rotations and Gaussian noise to translations, independently per residue per frame — then uses a single neural network to learn the joint reverse denoising.

Proposed Work

Objectives & Specific Aims

Hypothesis: A trajectory-level SE(3) diffusion model with explicit temporal attention can learn the joint distribution over protein conformational trajectories, reproducing both the equilibrium ensemble and the kinetic properties of molecular dynamics at a fraction of the computational cost.

1

Develop the MDGen Architecture

Design and implement a trajectory-level SE(3) diffusion model combining ESM-2 sequence embeddings, Invariant Point Attention (IPA) for spatial reasoning, and axial temporal attention for kinetics. Train on the ATLAS dataset.

2

Evaluate Equilibrium and Kinetic Fidelity

Benchmark against MD ground truth using RMSF Pearson correlation, Ramachandran JSD, frame RMSD distributions, and autocorrelation functions. Compare against NMA, AlphaFlow, and BioEmu baselines.

3

Demonstrate Downstream Applications

Apply MDGen to (a) cryptic pocket discovery for drug design, (b) conformational ensemble generation for intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs), and (c) mutational effect prediction on protein flexibility.

Overall objective: A single generative model that emulates protein MD at ~10,000× speedup, enabling routinely inaccessible conformational sampling for drug design, protein engineering, and fundamental biophysics.

Approach — Aim 1

Architecture Overview

MDGen is an encoder–processor–decoder network with three specialised components.

1

ESM-2 Sequence Encoder — Pre-trained 150M protein language model. Extracts per-residue embeddings si ∈ ℝd encoding evolutionary context and biochemical properties. Weights frozen during trajectory training.

2

Spatial IPA Layers — Invariant Point Attention operates in 3-D space: attention weights combine standard dot-products with 3-D point distances and orientations. Processes each frame independently → SE(3)-equivariant updates to (Ri, ti).

3

Temporal Attention Layers — After spatial IPA, axial attention is applied across the time dimension of the F-frame window. Each residue attends to its own state at all other time steps, learning kinetic correlations.

4

Output Heads — Separate prediction heads for translation score ∈ ℝ³, rotation score ∈ so(3), and torsion angles. Combined into a joint denoising step via the training loss.

Slide 13 of 22
Approach — Key Innovation

Temporal Attention: The Core Novelty

Standard SE(3) diffusion models process each frame independently. MDGen introduces axial temporal attention to couple frames.

  • Axial attention: factored attention — first attend spatially (residue–residue), then temporally (frame–frame) — linear cost O(L + F) vs. O(L·F)
  • For each residue i at frame f, compute attention keys/queries/values from all frames f′ ∈ [1,F]
  • Result: the network learns that large displacement at frame f is followed by smaller displacement (reversion) at frame f+1 — correct kinetics
\( \mathbf{h}_i^f \leftarrow \text{Attn}_\text{time}\!\left(\mathbf{h}_i^{f'},\, f' = 1\ldots F\right) \)
Why this works: Autocorrelation C(τ) = ⟨δx(t)·δx(t+τ)⟩ decays exponentially in MD. Temporal attention learns this decay from data — i.i.d. frame models cannot.

Approach — Training Data

ATLAS Dataset

MDGen is trained on the ATLAS open-access MD database — the largest curated protein dynamics benchmark available.

  • 1,390 proteins spanning diverse folds: all-α, all-β, α/β, disordered
  • 300 ns × 3 replicates per protein — sampled at 100 ps → 3,000 frames per protein
  • AMBER ff14SB force field + TIP3P water + NPT ensemble at 310 K
  • Train/validation/test split: 1,143 / 100 / 147 proteins (sequence-similarity stratified)
Trajectory windowing: During training, random 16-frame windows of ~1 ns each are sampled from 300 ns trajectories — providing diverse kinetic contexts and augmenting effective training set size.

The diversity of protein sizes (50–700 residues) and fold types ensures generalisation across the protein universe.

Approach — Training

Training Objective

The total loss combines four terms, each targeting a different geometric component of the backbone representation.

Translation Score Loss

\( \mathcal{L}_t = \mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\hat{\mathbf{s}}_t - \mathbf{s}_t\|^2\bigr] \)

MSE between predicted and true translation score (Gaussian score = scaled displacement)

Rotation Score Loss (Riemannian)

\( \mathcal{L}_r = \mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\hat{\mathbf{s}}_R - \mathbf{s}_R\|^2_{T_{R}SO(3)}\bigr] \)

Score matching in the tangent space of SO(3) — ensures geodesically correct rotation updates

Torsion Score Loss

\( \mathcal{L}_\chi = \mathbb{E}\bigl[\|\hat{\mathbf{s}}_\chi - \mathbf{s}_\chi\|^2_{\mathbb{T}^k}\bigr] \)

Periodic score matching on the torus for backbone dihedral angles φ, ψ and sidechain χ angles

Violation Penalty

\( \mathcal{L}_v = \lambda \cdot \text{clash}(\hat{x}) + \mu \cdot \text{bond}(\hat{x}) \)

Soft geometric constraints penalising steric clashes and bond-length violations in generated structures

Total loss: \(\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_t + \mathcal{L}_r + \mathcal{L}_\chi + \mathcal{L}_v\) — trained for 100k steps on 8×A100 GPUs (~5 days)

Results — Aim 2

Evaluation Metrics

We use five complementary metrics to assess both equilibrium and kinetic fidelity.

RMSF Pearson r Per-residue Root-Mean-Square Fluctuation correlation with MD. Primary equilibrium metric — measures flexibility matching.
Ramachandran JSD Jensen–Shannon Divergence between predicted and MD φ-ψ distributions. Tests backbone geometry correctness.
Frame RMSD Distribution of frame-to-frame Cα displacements. A kinetic metric — wrong if frames are i.i.d. sampled.
Autocorrelation C(τ) Temporal correlation ⟨δx(t)·δx(t+τ)⟩. Must decay exponentially — only trajectory-level models can reproduce this.
Crucial distinction: Independent frame samplers (AlphaFlow, BioEmu) can achieve good RMSF and Ramachandran scores but always fail on frame RMSD and autocorrelation — they produce random orderings, not physical sequences of frames.

Application — Aim 3a

Cryptic Pocket Discovery

Cryptic pockets are drug-binding sites invisible in the static X-ray structure — they only open transiently during conformational fluctuations.

  • Traditional drug discovery requires microsecond MD to reveal these pockets — months of computation per target
  • MDGen generates 100–1,000 conformations in seconds, capturing the pocket-opening events
  • Demonstrated on Interleukin-2 (IL-2): MDGen recovers the known cryptic pocket with correct geometry and frequency
Impact: Enables high-throughput virtual screening against conformationally-gated targets — could accelerate hit discovery by months per target.
Limitation: Fixed 16-frame (~1 ns) window means very slow transitions (rare events on ms timescale) are not directly accessible.
Slide 19 of 22
Application — Aim 3b

Intrinsically Disordered Proteins

IDPs lack a stable 3-D fold yet are critical in signalling, regulation, and disease. They resist crystallography and are notoriously hard to simulate.

  • α-synuclein (Parkinson's disease): disordered monomer that aggregates into toxic fibrils — MDGen captures broad ensemble without a defined fold
  • Tau (Alzheimer's disease): 441-residue repeat-domain IDP; MDGen samples native-like contact patterns
  • p53 transactivation domain: coupled folding-upon-binding; MDGen captures pre-formed helices present in partially disordered ensemble
Key finding: MDGen generates broad, experimentally consistent ensembles for IDPs without retraining — indicating the model has learned general principles of disordered dynamics.

Outlook

Limitations & Future Directions

MDGen is a major advance, but several limitations define the roadmap for future work.

Current Limitation

Fixed 16-frame window (~1 ns): rare events on ms timescales not directly accessible. Stitching windows introduces discontinuities.

Next step: Hierarchical latent dynamics model; predict coarse-grained transitions, then refine with MDGen.

Current Limitation

Backbone-only: sidechains and explicit water omitted. Drug binding and enzyme catalysis require full-atom accuracy.

Next step: Extend output to all-atom representation using a sidechain prediction head (e.g., Rosetta Packer or DiffPack).

Current Limitation

Training distribution bias: ATLAS covers single-domain soluble proteins; membrane proteins and large complexes excluded.

Next step: Curate membrane-protein MD datasets; add lipid bilayer conditioning.

Future Direction

Protein–ligand dynamics: Condition on bound ligand structure to generate drug-protein dynamics trajectories relevant for binding kinetics (kon, koff).

Future Direction

Experimental conditioning: Incorporate HDX-MS or SAXS data as soft constraints during sampling — model guided by sparse experimental observables.

Future Direction

Protein–protein complexes: Extend to dimers and oligomers; model interface dynamics for antibody–antigen and signalling complex design.

Slide 21 of 22
Conclusions

Summary

MDGen addresses a fundamental bottleneck in structural biology — the gap between what we can simulate and what matters biologically.

1 — The Problem

Protein conformational dynamics govern function, but MD is limited by cost and timescale. Existing generative models sample equilibrium but ignore kinetics.

2 — The Innovation

MDGen is the first trajectory-level SE(3) diffusion model. Axial temporal attention is the key novelty enabling physically correct autocorrelation and frame-to-frame statistics.

3 — The Evidence

Preliminary results: RMSF r = 0.87, Ramachandran JSD = 0.08, correct ACF — all at ~10,000× speedup vs. MD on 1,390 proteins.

MDGen turns a weeks-long MD simulation into a seconds-long generative inference — democratising conformational sampling for drug design, protein engineering, and biophysics.
