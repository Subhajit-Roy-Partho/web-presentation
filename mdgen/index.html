Biology background 1

Proteins: the molecular machines of life

Proteins are linear chains of amino acids that fold into precise three-dimensional shapes. Their 3D structure determines their biological function — from catalysing chemical reactions to transmitting signals across cell membranes.

Key fact: there are 20 standard amino acids. A protein of 100 residues can form an astronomical number of possible sequences — yet evolution has found sequences that reliably fold into a specific functional shape.
  • Primary structure: the amino-acid sequence (1D).
  • Secondary structure: local patterns — α-helices, β-strands.
  • Tertiary structure: the full 3D fold.
Introductory protein biochemistry; see Branden & Tooze, Introduction to Protein Structure, 1999.

Biology background 2

The backbone: what MDGen actually models

The protein backbone is the repeating N–Cα–C=O chain that runs through all amino acids. Only the side chains branching from Cα differ between residues.

MDGen operates on backbone frames — one rigid frame per residue anchored at N, Cα, C. Side chains can be rebuilt separately after generating the backbone trajectory.
  • Backbone (~4 heavy atoms per residue) captures the gross fold and large-scale dynamics.
  • The backbone dihedral angles φ and ψ encode almost all backbone conformation.
  • Domain swings, loop openings, and hinge motions are all fully visible at backbone level.
Backbone frame representation: Jumper et al., AlphaFold, Nature 2021; MDGen, Jing et al. 2024.

Biology background 3

A protein is a distribution, not a single shape

Experimental structures give one representative conformation. But in solution at body temperature, a protein continuously fluctuates among many nearby states. The biologically relevant object is an ensemble — a probability distribution over conformations.

\[ p(x) \propto \exp\!\left(-\frac{E(x)}{k_B T}\right) \]
  • \(x\): a molecular conformation (set of atomic coordinates).
  • \(E(x)\): the potential energy of that conformation.
  • \(k_B T \approx 0.6\) kcal/mol at 300 K — the thermal energy scale.
  • Flexible loops, domain motions, and cryptic pockets all emerge from this distribution.
Boltzmann distribution in molecular biology: Adcock & McCammon, Chem. Rev. 2006; MDGen motivation, Jing et al. 2024.

Biology background 4

Molecular dynamics: sampling motion by simulation

MD numerically integrates Newton's equations of motion for every atom in the system. At each tiny time step (2 fs = 2×10⁻¹⁵ s), forces are computed from a force field and atomic positions are updated.

\[ m_i \frac{d^2\mathbf{r}_i}{dt^2} = \mathbf{F}_i = -\nabla_{\mathbf{r}_i} E(\mathbf{r}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{r}_N) \]

The force field \(E\) includes bonded terms (bonds, angles, dihedrals) and non-bonded terms (Lennard-Jones van der Waals + electrostatics). Widely used packages: AMBER, GROMACS, NAMD, OpenMM.

MDGen's role: MDGen does not replace the force field — it learns from MD trajectories and emulates their statistical output without re-running the expensive simulation.
MD simulation overview: Adcock & McCammon, Chem. Rev. 2006; force field context: AMBER ff14SB, CHARMM36m.

The core problem

The timescale gap: why MD is expensive

The fastest atomic vibrations (bond stretches ~10 fs) force tiny time steps, while biologically important motions happen microseconds to milliseconds later. This gap spans 9–12 orders of magnitude.

Bond vibration

~10 fs per oscillation. Sets the MD time step ceiling at 2 fs (with constraints).

Domain motion / folding

μs–ms. Requires 10⁸–10¹¹ integration steps to observe directly.

A single microsecond simulation of a 100-residue protein on a modern GPU cluster can take days to weeks. Screening thousands of protein variants is impractical.
Timescale problem in MD: Shaw et al., Science 2010; ATLAS dataset: Vander Meersche et al., NAR 2024.

ML prerequisite 1

Diffusion models: learn to reverse noise

Diffusion models define a forward process that gradually adds Gaussian noise to data until it becomes pure noise, then learn a reverse process to denoise — generating new data from noise.

\[ q(\mathbf{x}_t \mid \mathbf{x}_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_t;\;\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf{x}_0,\;(1-\bar\alpha_t)\mathbf{I}\right) \]
  • \(\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s=1}^t (1-\beta_s)\): cumulative noise schedule.
  • At \(t=T\): \(\mathbf{x}_T \approx \mathcal{N}(0,\mathbf{I})\) — pure noise.
  • The neural network \(\epsilon_\theta\) learns to predict the noise \(\epsilon\) added at each step.
  • Sampling: start from noise, apply the learned reverse process iteratively.
Denoising diffusion probabilistic models: Ho et al., NeurIPS 2020; Song et al., ICLR 2021.

ML prerequisite 2

Score matching: the continuous-time view

Diffusion can be framed as a stochastic differential equation (SDE). The score function \(\nabla_\mathbf{x} \log p_t(\mathbf{x})\) points toward regions of higher data density — it is the key quantity the neural network learns to approximate.

\[ d\mathbf{x} = f(\mathbf{x},t)\,dt + g(t)\,d\mathbf{W} \quad\xrightarrow{\text{reverse}}\quad d\mathbf{x} = \bigl[f - g^2 \nabla\log p_t\bigr]dt + g\,d\bar{\mathbf{W}} \]
  • \(\mathbf{W}\): standard Brownian motion.
  • Forward SDE: diffuses data to noise. Reverse SDE: recovers data from noise.
  • The score network \(s_\theta(\mathbf{x},t) \approx \nabla_\mathbf{x}\log p_t(\mathbf{x})\) is trained via denoising score matching.
  • MDGen uses this framework, extended to the non-Euclidean SE(3) geometry of backbone frames.
Score-based generative models: Song et al., ICLR 2021; denoising score matching: Vincent 2011.

ML prerequisite 3

SE(3) equivariance: coordinates shouldn't matter

A protein's 3D coordinates depend on an arbitrary lab frame: we could rotate or translate the whole molecule without changing any physics. A model that respects this symmetry is called SE(3)-equivariant.

\[ f(g \cdot \mathbf{x}) = \rho_{\text{out}}(g)\,f(\mathbf{x}), \quad g \in SE(3) \]
  • Invariant output (e.g., energy): \(f(g\cdot\mathbf{x}) = f(\mathbf{x})\). Unchanged by rotation/translation.
  • Equivariant output (e.g., forces, coordinates): rotates consistently with input.
  • SE(3) = SO(3) rotations × ℝ³ translations in 3D space.
  • MDGen's score network is SE(3)-equivariant: generating coordinates in a different lab frame gives the same trajectory, just rotated.
SE(3)-equivariant networks: Fuchs et al., SE(3)-Transformers, NeurIPS 2020; Thomas et al., TFN; EGNN: Satorras et al., ICML 2021.

ML prerequisite 4

Representing the backbone as SE(3) frames

MDGen and related models (FrameDiff, AlphaFold2 structure module) represent each residue as a rigid body in SE(3): a rotation R (local backbone orientation) and a translation t (Cα position).

\[ \mathbf{x} = \{(R_i, \mathbf{t}_i)\}_{i=1}^{N} \in SE(3)^N,\quad R_i\in SO(3),\;\mathbf{t}_i\in\mathbb{R}^3 \]

For a trajectory of T frames, the full state is:

\[ \mathbf{X} = (\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_T) \in SE(3)^{N\times T} \]
  • Side chains are encoded separately as torsion angles χ₁–χ₄ on the torus \(\mathbb{T}^4\).
  • This factored representation enables separate, geometrically correct diffusion on each component.
Frame representation: AlphaFold structure module (Jumper et al. 2021); FrameDiff (Yim et al. 2023); MDGen (Jing et al. 2024).

ML prerequisite 5

Diffusion on non-Euclidean spaces: SO(3) and the torus

Rotations live on SO(3) — a curved manifold, not flat Euclidean space. Standard Gaussian diffusion doesn't apply directly. MDGen uses the isotropic Gaussian on SO(3) (IGSO3) distribution.

\[ q(R_t \mid R_0) = \mathrm{IGSO3}(R_t;\; R_0,\; \sigma_t^2) \]

The score on SO(3) uses the Riemannian gradient — the analogue of \(\nabla_x\log p\) on curved space. For torsion angles (on circle \(S^1\)), a wrapped Gaussian is used:

\[ q(\theta_t\mid\theta_0)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\mathcal{N}(\theta_t;\,\theta_0+2\pi k,\,\sigma_t^2) \]
IGSO3 diffusion: De Bortoli et al., NeurIPS 2022; Leach et al. 2022; FrameDiff (Yim et al. 2023); MDGen (Jing et al. 2024).

Key insight

Why trajectories require more than independent samples

Prior protein diffusion models (RFdiffusion, FrameDiff, EigenFold) generate independent structure samples — each frame is drawn independently from the equilibrium distribution. This misses everything about how the protein moves over time.

Independent sampling

Correct equilibrium distribution. Zero temporal coherence: consecutive frames can jump anywhere — like random teleportation. Cannot reproduce autocorrelation functions or kinetic timescales.

MDGen trajectories

Temporally coherent: consecutive frames are similar (small physical steps). Reproduces autocorrelation functions, correct step sizes, and kinetic information encoded in the trajectory.

MDGen's core novelty: it is the first model to generate full-protein all-backbone trajectories with learned temporal structure — not just equilibrium ensembles.
Trajectory vs. ensemble generation distinction: MDGen paper, Jing et al. 2024, Section 1 and Appendix C.

MDGen method 1

MDGen: the generative problem statement

Given a protein sequence \(\mathbf{s}\), generate a trajectory \(\mathbf{X} = (\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_T)\) where each frame \(\mathbf{x}_t \in SE(3)^N\) is a physically plausible, temporally coherent backbone conformation.

\[ p_\theta(\mathbf{X} \mid \mathbf{s}) = p_\theta(\mathbf{x}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{x}_T \mid \mathbf{s}) \]

The model should satisfy two simultaneous goals:

1

Equilibrium correctness: the marginal distribution of any single frame \(p(\mathbf{x}_t)\) matches the MD equilibrium ensemble.

2

Temporal coherence: consecutive frames \((\mathbf{x}_t, \mathbf{x}_{t+1})\) are correlated as in real MD — the conditional \(p(\mathbf{x}_{t+1}\mid\mathbf{x}_t)\) reflects physical dynamics.

MDGen problem formulation: Jing et al., arXiv:2409.17808, Section 2.

MDGen method 2

Forward diffusion over the full trajectory

MDGen noises each frame independently across diffusion time \(s\). The forward process factorises over frames (but not over residues within a frame):

\[ q(\mathbf{X}^{(s)} \mid \mathbf{X}^{(0)}) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} q(\mathbf{x}_t^{(s)} \mid \mathbf{x}_t^{(0)}) \]

Within each frame, diffusion is applied jointly to translations (in ℝ³), rotations (on SO(3)), and torsion angles (on \(\mathbb{T}^4\)):

\[ q(\mathbf{x}_t^{(s)} \mid \mathbf{x}_t^{(0)}) = q_{\mathbb{R}^3}(\mathbf{t}^{(s)}\mid\mathbf{t}^{(0)})\cdot q_{SO(3)}(R^{(s)}\mid R^{(0)})\cdot q_{\mathbb{T}}(\boldsymbol\chi^{(s)}\mid\boldsymbol\chi^{(0)}) \]

At maximum noise level \(s=S\): all frames are independent noise. The reverse process must simultaneously denoise all T frames while learning their temporal correlations.

MDGen forward diffusion formulation: Jing et al. 2024, Section 2; IGSO3: De Bortoli et al. 2022.

MDGen method 3

Neural architecture: IPA + temporal attention

The denoising network \(\epsilon_\theta\) processes all T noisy frames simultaneously. It stacks two types of attention layers in alternation:

1

Sequence encoder (ESM-2): the protein sequence is embedded by a frozen protein language model into per-residue feature vectors \(\mathbf{s}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d\).

2

Spatial IPA layers: at each trajectory frame, Invariant Point Attention exchanges information across residues using the 3D backbone geometry.

3

Temporal attention layers: for each residue position, attention is applied across the time axis — frames at \(t=1,\ldots,T\) attend to each other.

4

Score head: outputs predicted noise \(\hat\epsilon\) for translations (ℝ³), rotations (SO(3)), and torsions (\(\mathbb{T}^4\)) at each residue and frame.

MDGen architecture: Jing et al. 2024, Section 3; IPA: Jumper et al. 2021 (AlphaFold2 structure module).

MDGen method 4

Invariant Point Attention: geometry-aware attention

Introduced in AlphaFold2, IPA augments standard attention with 3D query/key points expressed in each residue's local frame. The attention score between residues \(i\) and \(j\) includes a geometry term:

\[ a_{ij} = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{q_i k_j^\top}{\sqrt{d}} - \frac{\gamma}{2}\sum_h \|T_i \mathbf{p}_i^h - T_j \mathbf{p}_j^h\|^2\right) \]
  • \(T_i = (R_i, \mathbf{t}_i)\): the rigid frame of residue \(i\).
  • \(\mathbf{p}_i^h\): learned query/key 3D points in residue \(i\)'s local frame.
  • \(T_i \mathbf{p}_i^h\): transforms points to global frame for comparison.
  • The attention is SE(3)-invariant: rotating/translating all frames leaves scores unchanged.
  • Enables the model to reason about spatial proximity — two distant sequence residues that are close in 3D space will have a high attention weight.
Invariant Point Attention: Jumper et al., AlphaFold2, Nature 2021, Supplement Methods; MDGen reuses IPA for spatial attention.

MDGen method 5 — key innovation

Temporal attention: learning to connect frames

The critical novelty in MDGen is axial attention applied over the temporal dimension. After spatial IPA across residues (at fixed time), a second attention pass runs across time for each residue:

\[ \mathbf{h}_{i,t}^{\text{new}} = \sum_{t'=1}^{T} \alpha_{tt'}\,\mathbf{v}_{i,t'} \quad\text{where}\quad \alpha_{tt'} = \mathrm{softmax}_{{t'}}\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}_{i,t}\cdot\mathbf{k}_{i,t'}}{\sqrt{d}}\right) \]
  • For residue \(i\), attention is computed across all T frames in the trajectory.
  • This allows information to flow from any time step to any other — the model can look ahead and look back within a trajectory window.
  • The temporal context is what makes MDGen's generated trajectories coherent: neighbouring frames share information through these temporal attention weights.
  • This is analogous to axial attention in image models (row/column attention), adapted to the protein trajectory (residue/time) domain.
Temporal axial attention: MDGen paper, Jing et al. 2024, Section 3.2; axial attention: Ho et al. 2019 (images); extended to trajectories here.

MDGen method 6

Training: denoising score matching over trajectory space

The training objective is denoising score matching applied jointly to all T frames and all three geometric components. The total loss sums over translations, rotations, and torsions:

\[ \mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{s,\mathbf{X}^{(0)},\mathbf{X}^{(s)}}\!\left[\, \lambda_{\text{trans}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{R}^3} + \lambda_{\text{rot}}\mathcal{L}_{SO(3)} + \lambda_{\text{tors}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{T}} + \lambda_{\text{viol}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{viol}}\right] \]
  • \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{R}^3} = \|\hat\epsilon - \epsilon\|^2\): standard MSE for translations.
  • \(\mathcal{L}_{SO(3)}\): Riemannian score matching loss on the SO(3) manifold.
  • \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathbb{T}}\): wrapped Gaussian score matching for torsion angles.
  • \(\mathcal{L}_{\text{viol}}\): violation penalty for steric clashes and bond geometry violations (weight ≈ 0.01).
Training data: ATLAS MD trajectories split into overlapping windows of T=16 frames. Diffusion time \(s \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,S)\) sampled each step.
Training loss: MDGen paper, Jing et al. 2024, Section 2.3; score matching: Song & Ermon 2019.

Data

The ATLAS MD database: training data for MDGen

ATLAS (Atlas of Trajectory Landscapes and Associated Simulations) is a large-scale, curated MD database assembled specifically to support machine learning on protein dynamics.

3 × 100 nsreplicas per protein (300 ns total)
~1.2 μstotal aggregate simulation time
  • Force field: AMBER ff14SB with explicit TIP3P water, NPT ensemble at 300 K, 1 atm.
  • Frames stored: every 1 ns — ~300 frames per protein.
  • Coverage: diverse fold space including α, β, α/β, intrinsically disordered regions.
  • Training split: ~1,265 training proteins; ~135 held-out test proteins.
ATLAS database: Vander Meersche et al., Nucleic Acids Research 2024; MDGen training details: Jing et al. 2024.

MDGen method 7

Training procedure: windows, noise, and gradient steps

1

Sample a protein from the ATLAS training set and pick a random trajectory window of T=16 consecutive frames.

2

Sample diffusion time \(s \sim \mathrm{Uniform}(0,S)\), where S=500 steps defines the noise schedule.

3

Add noise to all T frames simultaneously using IGSO3, Gaussian, and wrapped Gaussian forward processes.

4

Run the network \(\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{X}^{(s)}, s, \mathbf{s})\) on all T noisy frames — spatial and temporal attention layers process the full window.

5

Compute and backpropagate the total loss \(\mathcal{L}\). Optimiser: AdamW, learning rate 1e-4 with cosine decay.

Training details: MDGen paper, Jing et al. 2024, Section 4 (implementation); AdamW: Loshchilov & Hutter 2019.

MDGen method 8

Inference: generating trajectories from noise

Sampling runs the reverse diffusion starting from pure noise for all T frames simultaneously. MDGen supports both DDPM (500 steps) and accelerated DDIM (50 steps) sampling:

1

Initialise: draw pure noise \(\mathbf{X}^{(S)} \sim p_{\text{noise}}\) — translations from \(\mathcal{N}(0,I)\), rotations from Uniform(SO(3)), torsions from Uniform(\(\mathbb{T}^4\)).

2

Condition on sequence: embed protein sequence with ESM-2 once; reuse across all reverse steps.

3

Reverse loop: for \(s = S, S-1, \ldots, 1\): run \(\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf{X}^{(s)}, s)\) and update \(\mathbf{X}^{(s-1)}\) via the reverse SDE/ODE.

4

Output: \(\mathbf{X}^{(0)} = (\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_T)\) — a physically realistic, temporally coherent protein trajectory.

DDIM reduces sampling steps from 500 to ~50 with minimal quality loss — enabling batch trajectory generation in seconds on one GPU.
Sampling algorithm: MDGen paper, Jing et al. 2024, Section 2.4; DDIM: Song et al., ICLR 2021.

MDGen method 9

Trajectory extension via diffusion inpainting

MDGen can be conditioned on a known starting conformation using a technique borrowed from image inpainting: during reverse diffusion, the known frame is always set to its correctly-noised version, constraining the sampled trajectory to start from a specific state.

\[ \mathbf{x}_1^{(s)} \leftarrow \sqrt{\bar\alpha_s}\,\mathbf{x}_1^{(0)} + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_s}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon, \quad\text{(inpainted)} \]

This enables three powerful inference modes:

Extension

Condition on frame \(\mathbf{x}_1\) to generate a continuation from a known crystal structure or AlphaFold prediction.

Interpolation

Condition on both \(\mathbf{x}_1\) and \(\mathbf{x}_T\) to generate a plausible transition pathway between two known conformational states.

Conditional sampling

Given any partial trajectory observations, fill in the missing frames consistently with the observed ones.

Inpainting for trajectory conditioning: MDGen paper, Jing et al. 2024, Section 2.5; repaint framework: Lugmayr et al. 2022.

Evaluation 1

RMSF: measuring per-residue flexibility

Root Mean Square Fluctuation (RMSF) is the primary metric for protein flexibility. It measures how much each residue's position deviates from its time-averaged position across the trajectory.

\[ \mathrm{RMSF}_i = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\|\mathbf{r}_i(t) - \langle\mathbf{r}_i\rangle\|^2} \]
  • High RMSF → flexible region (loops, termini, disordered regions).
  • Low RMSF → rigid region (helices, β-sheets, hydrophobic core).
  • Biologically meaningful: RMSF pattern encodes which parts of the protein are dynamic vs. rigid.
Evaluation: MDGen's RMSF per-residue profile is compared to reference MD using Pearson correlation \(r\). A good model should reproduce which residues are flexible, not just the average flexibility.
RMSF metric: standard in MD evaluation; MDGen RMSF results: Jing et al. 2024, Section 4.1.

Evaluation 2

Temporal metrics: autocorrelation and step sizes

Two metrics specifically test temporal coherence — quantities that are impossible to evaluate from an independent sample generator:

Autocorrelation function C(τ)

Measures how correlated the structure is with itself after a lag of τ frames. In real MD, C(τ) decays smoothly — fast decorrelation on ps timescales, slower on ns scales.

\[C(\tau) = \frac{\langle\mathbf{x}(t)\cdot\mathbf{x}(t+\tau)\rangle}{\langle\mathbf{x}(t)^2\rangle}\]

Frame-to-frame RMSD

In real MD, consecutive frames differ by only a tiny amount (~0.1–0.3 Å Cα RMSD). Independent frame generators show arbitrarily large jumps. MDGen reproduces the physical step-size distribution.

\[\Delta_t = \|\mathbf{x}(t+1) - \mathbf{x}(t)\|_{\mathrm{RMSD}}\]
These temporal metrics directly measure whether MDGen has learned dynamics, not just equilibrium statistics. Only MDGen can pass them; independent samplers cannot.
Temporal coherence evaluation: MDGen paper, Jing et al. 2024, Section 4.2; ACF metric: standard in MD analysis.

Evaluation 3

Ramachandran plots: backbone dihedral quality

The Ramachandran plot displays the joint distribution of backbone dihedral angles (φ, ψ) for all residues across an ensemble. Physically realistic conformations populate specific regions — α-helical, β-strand, and left-handed helical.

If a generative model produces unphysical backbone geometries (e.g. populating "disallowed" Ramachandran regions), the backbone is physically unrealistic regardless of other metrics.

MDGen is evaluated on:

  • Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD) between generated and reference (φ,ψ) distributions across all residues in test proteins.
  • Lower JSD = better agreement. MDGen achieves JSD ~0.08 vs reference; NMA achieves ~0.19.
  • Side-chain torsion (χ₁, χ₂) distributions are also compared using rotamer population fractions.
Ramachandran analysis: standard protein structure quality metric; MDGen Ramachandran results: Jing et al. 2024, Section 4.1.

Results — comparisons

How MDGen compares to existing methods

MDGen is positioned among several categories of prior work, each with different strengths and weaknesses:

Normal Mode Analysis

Classical harmonic model. Fast but only captures small vibrations around equilibrium — misses barrier crossings, large-amplitude motions, and anharmonic effects. RMSF r ≈ 0.64.

Independent diffusion

FrameDiff/EigenFold-style: generates each frame independently from the equilibrium distribution. No temporal coherence. RMSF r ≈ 0.71 but fails all temporal metrics.

Boltzmann generators

Normalizing-flow-based equilibrium samplers. Good for small molecules; challenging to scale to full proteins. No temporal order — produce independent equilibrium samples.

RFdiffusion / ESMFold

Static structure generators. Provide one structure; cannot produce ensembles or dynamics. Not designed for flexibility prediction.

AlphaFold2 subsampling

MSA depth subsampling produces alternative conformations, but the variance is limited and not calibrated to MD-like ensembles. Requires expensive MSA pipeline.

MDGen (this work)

First model generating temporally coherent full-protein backbone trajectories. Matches MD on both equilibrium (RMSF r ≈ 0.87) and temporal (ACF, Δ RMSD) metrics.

Comparison to baselines: MDGen paper, Jing et al. 2024, Section 4.3; NMA: Tirion 1996, Yang et al.; Boltzmann generators: Noé et al. 2019.

Results — challenging test

Challenging test: fast-folding miniproteins

Fast-folding proteins (Trp-cage, BBA motif, villin headpiece, WW domain, NTL9, Chignolin) are classic MD benchmarks — they fold on microsecond timescales and have rich conformational heterogeneity accessible to long MD simulations.

These proteins were not in the ATLAS training set. Testing MDGen on them probes generalisation beyond the training distribution. Results show MDGen captures the correct flexibility patterns even on unseen proteins.
  • Correctly identifies highly flexible N- and C-terminal regions.
  • Reproduces the relative flexibility of loops vs. secondary structure.
  • Captures partial unfolding events in intrinsically flexible proteins.
  • Qualitative agreement with known folding-related flexibility hotspots.
Note: MDGen does not observe full folding/unfolding transitions within a 16-frame window (≈ 16 ns). Millisecond-scale rare events are outside its current scope.
Fast-folding protein benchmarks: Lindorff-Larsen et al., Science 2011; MDGen generalisation test: Jing et al. 2024, Section 4.4.

Application 1

Drug discovery: ensembles reveal hidden pockets

The most direct application of MDGen to drug discovery is cryptic pocket detection: binding sites that are absent in the static crystal structure but transiently open during protein dynamics.

Why this matters: ~50% of protein drug targets undergo conformational changes critical for inhibitor binding. Static docking against a single structure misses these pockets entirely. MDGen-generated ensembles expose them in seconds.
  • Induced fit: drug reshapes the pocket as it binds — ensemble docking captures this.
  • Allosteric sites: conformational signalling from a distant site; MDGen captures the coupled motion.
  • Virtual screening: dock against MDGen ensemble instead of one static structure → reduced false-negative rate.
  • Workflow: generate 100–1000 MDGen trajectory frames → run FPOCKET or SiteMap on each → identify transiently open druggable pockets.
Cryptic pockets: Oleinikovas et al. 2016; conformational selection: Boehr et al., Nat Chem Biol 2009; ensemble docking: Totrov & Abagyan 2008.

Application 2

Intrinsically disordered proteins and disease

Intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs) have no stable 3D fold — they exist as dynamic ensembles. They are impossible to characterise with single-structure methods like AlphaFold2, but are medically critical:

α-synuclein

Central to Parkinson's disease aggregation. Its disordered ensemble determines the aggregation pathway. No single structure exists. MDGen can sample its conformational landscape.

Tau

Forms neurofibrillary tangles in Alzheimer's disease. The disordered regions that initiate aggregation require ensemble characterisation, not a single fold.

p53

The most frequently mutated gene in cancer. Its disordered N- and C-terminal regions mediate key protein–protein interactions that differ across its conformational ensemble.

MDGen's training on ATLAS (which includes flexible and partially disordered proteins) gives it a data-driven understanding of disordered-region dynamics — a capability that is absent in static structure predictors.
IDPs in disease: Uversky, 2019; α-synuclein: Bhatt et al. 2023; tau: Fitzpatrick et al. 2017; MDGen IDP application: Jing et al. 2024.

Context

MDGen in the protein ML landscape

The rapid advance of protein ML tools has created a rich ecosystem. MDGen occupies a unique niche: trajectory generation at the intersection of structure prediction, ensemble generation, and generative modelling.

Static structure prediction

AlphaFold2, ESMFold, RoseTTAFold: one sequence → one structure. Highly accurate but generates no dynamics. Used by MDGen as the starting structure input.

Single-frame ensemble (equilibrium)

EigenFold, AlphaFlow, ConfDiff, BioEmu: one sequence → multiple independent equilibrium samples. Good RMSF, no temporal information. Prior work that MDGen extends.

Design models

RFdiffusion, Chroma, ProteinSGM: one condition → one designed structure. Optimised for design, not dynamics. Not evaluated on MD ensemble metrics.

MDGen (this work)

One sequence → T-frame temporally coherent trajectory. Unique combination of SE(3) diffusion + temporal attention enabling both equilibrium AND kinetic accuracy.

ML landscape context: AlphaFold (Jumper et al. 2021), EigenFold (Jing et al. 2023), AlphaFlow (Jing et al. 2024), BioEmu (Lewis et al. 2025).

Critical reading

What MDGen does not solve

  • Fixed timescale: generates 16-frame windows (~16 ns equivalent). Millisecond-scale events (full folding, large domain translocations) are out of scope.
  • Training distribution: ATLAS covers mostly globular, single-chain soluble proteins. Membrane proteins, large complexes, and heavily modified proteins are underrepresented.
  • No explicit solvent: learns implicit solvent statistics; cannot model hydration shell dynamics or ion effects explicitly.
  • Force-field dependency: generated trajectories emulate AMBER ff14SB statistics, not the true quantum-mechanical potential.
  • Backbone only: no all-atom side-chain ensembles (though side chains can be grafted post-hoc with Rosetta/DLPacker).
  • Rare barrier crossings: conformational transitions requiring high energy barriers (microseconds+) are very unlikely to appear in 16-frame windows.
Limitations: MDGen paper, Jing et al. 2024, Section 5 (discussion) and supplementary material.

Open questions

Future directions: where MDGen leads

Full-atom side chains

Extend MDGen to jointly model backbone frames and side-chain χ torsions as a unified all-atom generative model — enabling direct computation of binding free energies and rotamer-level interactions.

Longer timescales

ATLAS covers ~300 ns. Training on microsecond datasets (D. E. Shaw Research's Anton trajectories) or using enhanced-sampling MD (metadynamics, REST2) would unlock rare transitions.

Protein–ligand dynamics

Extending to receptor–ligand complexes would enable ensemble-aware docking, binding pose ensemble prediction, and entropic contributions to binding affinity (ΔG = ΔH − TΔS).

Protein–protein interfaces

Modelling multi-chain trajectory dynamics for antibody-antigen pairs, signalling complexes, and transient interaction interfaces — beyond the single-chain ATLAS training set.

Custom force fields

The MDGen framework can be retrained on any MD dataset. Training on polarisable force fields, coarse-grained simulations, or quantum mechanics/molecular mechanics (QM/MM) trajectories is a natural extension.

Beyond proteins

RNA, DNA, carbohydrates, and lipid membranes all undergo important dynamics accessible to MD. The SE(3) trajectory diffusion framework is not protein-specific and can be adapted to other biomolecular classes.

Future directions: MDGen paper, Jing et al. 2024, Section 5; enhanced sampling MD: Valsson et al., Annu Rev Phys Chem 2016.

Teaching synthesis

How to explain MDGen in one minute

1

Proteins are not static objects — they constantly fluctuate between many conformations. Understanding these fluctuations is essential for drug design, disease biology, and protein engineering.

2

Molecular dynamics simulation captures these fluctuations by integrating Newton's equations millions of times — but each simulation can take days to weeks on a GPU cluster.

3

MDGen learns from a large database of MD trajectories (ATLAS). Given a protein sequence, it generates entire trajectory segments — not just snapshots, but temporally connected series of structures.

4

The key technical ingredient is combining SE(3)-equivariant diffusion (which respects 3D geometry) with temporal axial attention (which learns how consecutive frames should be related).

5

The result: trajectory generation ~10,000× faster than MD, with correct per-residue flexibility, correct backbone dihedral distributions, and correct temporal autocorrelations — all simultaneously.

Synthesis of Jing et al. 2024, Sections 1–5.

Conclusion

Key takeaways

Novel problem framing

MDGen reframes protein ensemble modelling as trajectory generation, not just equilibrium sampling. This distinction matters: only temporal models can reproduce kinetic properties like autocorrelation functions.

Technical contribution

Combining SE(3)-equivariant diffusion on backbone frames with temporal axial attention is the architectural innovation that enables jointly correct equilibrium and dynamic statistics.

Empirical result

RMSF Pearson r ≈ 0.87 (vs NMA ≈ 0.64), correct Ramachandran distributions, correct frame-to-frame step sizes, and matching autocorrelation functions — all at ~10,000× speed-up over MD.

MDGen's central message: by treating MD trajectories as the generative target — not just equilibrium samples — we can learn both the thermodynamics AND the dynamics of protein motion from data.
Jing B, Stärk H, Jaakkola T, Berger B. Generative Molecular Dynamics Simulation. arXiv:2409.17808. NeurIPS 2024.
