fastDNA: Flow Matching for DNA Origami 3D Structure Prediction

A flow-matching generative model for predicting 3D coordinates of DNA nanostructures in the oxDNA2 coarse-grained representation — from random noise to physically grounded geometry in a single forward pass.

Motivation

DNA origami: programmable nanomachines in need of fast structure prediction

DNA origami folds a long scaffold strand into precise 3D nanoscale objects using hundreds of short staple strands as molecular staples. These structures are programmable, biocompatible, and nanometer-precise — making them powerful platforms for nanotechnology.

Drug delivery

DNA cages can encapsulate therapeutic cargo and release it in response to molecular triggers, enabling targeted cancer therapy.

Nanoscale robotics

Reconfigurable DNA structures act as molecular actuators, tweezers, and logic gates for computation and sensing.

Structural biology

Origami scaffolds position proteins and molecules at defined locations for cryo-EM analysis and single-molecule experiments.

The bottleneck: verifying that a designed sequence folds into the intended 3D geometry currently requires expensive oxDNA2 molecular dynamics simulations (hours to days per structure). fastDNA aims to replace this with a neural forward pass — milliseconds per structure.
DNA origami: Rothemund, Nature 2006; drug delivery: Banerjee et al. 2018; oxDNA2: Snodin et al. JCP 2015.

Background

DNA origami: scaffold, staples, and the oxDNA2 representation

DNA origami works by routing a long single-stranded scaffold through every designed helix, then using short staple strands to crosslink adjacent helices. The resulting shape is determined entirely by the staple sequences.

  • Scaffold strand: 1950–24000 nt, typically the M13mp18 phage genome (~7249 nt)
  • Staple strands: 50–200 strands, each 30–60 nt, binding two scaffold segments
  • oxDNA2 representation: each nucleotide = 1 centre-of-mass position + 1 orientation matrix
Each nucleotide: \(\mathbf{r} \in \mathbb{R}^3\) (COM position) + \(R \in SO(3)\) (3×3 orientation) \[R = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a}_1^\top \\ \mathbf{a}_2^\top \\ \mathbf{a}_3^\top \end{bmatrix}\]

a₁: base-pairing direction (backbone→base)
a₂: groove direction (= a₃ × a₁)
a₃: stacking axis (along helix)

Units: 1 simulation unit (su) = 0.8518 nm. All distances and bond lengths in su.

oxDNA2 coarse-grained model: Snodin et al. JCP 2015; DNA origami: Rothemund, Nature 2006; scaffold routing: Veneziano et al. Science 2016.

Physics · Force Field

Modular smooth functions: f1, f4, f5 — the building blocks of every interaction

All seven oxDNA2 energy terms are products of three types of smooth, differentiable functions. Smooth continuity ensures energy and force are well-behaved everywhere — critical for both MD stability and PyTorch autodiff.

f1(r) — smooth radial well (used in stacking, HB, cross-stacking): \[f_1(r) = \begin{cases} 0 & r > r^*_\text{high} \\ e^{-k_1(r-r_0)^2}\text{(ramp)} & r_\text{HB} \le r \le r^*_\text{high} \\ 1\text{ (plateau)} & r_\text{low} \le r \le r_\text{HB} \\ 0 & r < r_\text{low} \end{cases}\] f4(θ) — angular gate (6× in stacking, 6× in HB): \[f_4(\theta;\,\theta_0,k_4) = 1 - k_4(\cos\theta - \cos\theta_0)^2, \quad |\theta-\theta_0| \le \Delta\theta\] f5(φ) — groove/twist gate (stacking only): \[f_5(\phi;\,\phi_0) = \begin{cases} 1 & |\phi| \le |\phi_0| \\ 1 - k_5(\phi-\phi_0)^2 & |\phi_0| < |\phi| < |\phi_\text{cut}| \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}\]
Why smooth? Discontinuous cutoffs create energy drift in MD (and NaN gradients in PyTorch). The Gaussian rolloff in f1 and quadratic rolloff in f4/f5 ensure \(\nabla V\) is continuous everywhere. Our geom_loss surrogate uses squared differences without gates — giving gradient at all distances.
oxDNA2 smooth functions: Snodin et al. JCP 2015 Supp; DNA2Interaction.cpp functions _f1, _f4, _f5; surrogate in geom_loss.py uses ungated squared losses.

Physics · Stacking

Base stacking: helical twist φ is the key B-DNA geometry constraint

Stacking acts on bonded consecutive pairs on the same strand. Three geometric constraints are enforced, each verified against equilibrated BOILERPLATE dsDNA (504 nt).

stacking_geometry_loss (geom_loss.py): \[\mathcal{L}_\text{stck} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_\text{bonds}\left[5\,(\phi_\text{loss}) + 3\,(a_3\text{-par}) + 1\,(\theta_5\text{-align})\right]\] 1. Helical twist φ (weight×5) — the KEY constraint: \[|\cos\phi_1| = |a_2^p \cdot \hat{r}_\text{backref}| \to 0.656, \quad \cos\phi_1 = a_2 \cdot \frac{\Delta r_\text{COM} + d_\text{back}(a_1^q - a_1^p)}{|\cdot|}\] 2. a₃ nearly parallel (weight×3): \[a_3^p \cdot a_3^q \to 0.921 \approx \cos(22.9°)\] 3. Stack-site alignment |a₃·r̂| (weight×1): \[|a_3^p \cdot \hat{r}_\text{stack}| \to 0.932 \approx \cos(21.3°)\]
v0.6 bug, v0.9 fix: Old stacking used target a₃·a₃→0.809 (cos36°) from origami data that mixed strand directions. Correct target 0.921 (cos22.9°) from BOILERPLATE single-strand pairs. Also: POS_BACK=-0.4 (uses a₂, not a₃, for backbone reference — critical for correct φ). Absolute value handles both 5'→3' and 3'→5' strand storage in zarr.

Physics · Hydrogen Bonding

Watson-Crick H-bond: 5 smooth angular terms, all gates removed in v0.12

The HB geometry loss acts on sampled WC-compatible pairs (A↔T: base types 0+3=3; G↔C: types 2+1=3), enforcing five constraints simultaneously.

hb_geometry_loss (sampled, K=300 pairs/step): \[\mathcal{L}_\text{HB} = \frac{1}{M}\!\sum_\text{valid WC pairs}\!\left[4r_\text{term} + t_1 + t_2 + 3t_4 + 2t_5\right]\] where: \[r_\text{term} = (r_\text{base} - 0.40)^2\quad \text{[position gradient, no gate]}\] \[t_1 = (a_1^p\cdot\hat{r} - 1)^2,\quad t_2 = (a_1^q\cdot\hat{r} + 1)^2\] \[t_4 = (a_1^p\cdot a_1^q + 1)^2,\quad t_5 = (a_3^p\cdot a_3^q + 1)^2\]

Sampling: K=300 random i, seq_sep ≥ 4; WC check: bt[i]+bt[j]==3.

v0.12 fix (smooth losses): Old code used F.relu(1 - dot) gates that produce zero gradient when orientation is partly wrong. Replaced with smooth (dot - target)² everywhere → gradient at all orientations. Also: radial term r_term has NO distance gate → pulls WC partners into HB range even at large separation.

Physics · Nucleotide Attraction

WC attractor + hb_exc: attracting the right pairs while repelling all others

These two complementary losses solve a fundamental problem: bring each Watson-Crick base to its unique partner without causing other base-face contacts.

wc_attractor_loss — exclusive nearest-WC-partner pull: \[\mathcal{L}_\text{wc} = \frac{1}{N_\text{active}}\sum_{i}\,w_i\cdot\text{relu}(r_{ij} - r_\text{stop})^2 \cdot \mathbf{1}[r_{ij} < r_\text{attract}]\] where \(j=\arg\min_{k:\text{WC}(i,k)} r_{ik}\) (unique nearest WC partner),
\(w_i=1.0\) if mutual (i is also j's nearest WC partner), \(w_i=0.5\) otherwise.
\(r_\text{attract}=1.5\) su, \(r_\text{stop}=0.40\) su (= HYDR_R0)

hb_exc_loss_nonwc — base-face exclusion for non-WC pairs only: \[\mathcal{L}_\text{exc}^\text{HB} = e^{-d/\sigma} + 10\cdot\text{relu}(r_\text{clash}-d)^2, \quad \text{only if bt}[i]+\text{bt}[j] \ne 3\]
v0.12 fix (wc_mask): Old code compared bt[i] to comp[i] (a pair of the same nucleotide) — always true, so every pair was treated as WC-compatible. Fixed to bt[i]+bt[j]==3, verified on displaced dsDNA test where wc_attractor now correctly fires only on A-T and G-C cross-strand pairs.

Why mutual weighting (1.0 vs 0.5)? If j already has a better WC partner, pulling i toward j at full weight would cause two bases to compete for the same partner, producing collapsed clumps. Halving the weight for non-mutual pairs allows the best partner to "win" while still providing weak global pull.

wc_attractor_loss() and hb_exc_loss_nonwc(): geom_loss.py; WC types A(0)+T(3)=3, G(2)+C(1)=3; r_attract=1.5su, r_stop=0.40su; hb_exc σ=0.30su, r_clash=0.25su.

Physics · Groove Geometry

groove_geometry_loss: the only loss that gives positions a gradient from orientation constraints

Added in v0.12, groove_geometry_loss bridges the gap between orientation and position gradients. The first two sub-terms enforce only orientation; the backbone MM-site sub-term is the key position signal.

\[\mathcal{L}_\text{groove} = 2\,\mathcal{L}_{a_1\perp a_3} + 1\,\mathcal{L}_{a_2\text{-par}} + 3\,\mathcal{L}_\text{back}\] (a) a₁ ⊥ a₃ [orientation only]: \[\mathcal{L}_{a_1\perp a_3} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i (a_1^i \cdot a_3^i)^2 \to 0\] (b) Consecutive a₂ parallel [orientation only]: \[\mathcal{L}_{a_2} = \frac{1}{N_b}\sum_\text{bonds} (|a_2^p \cdot a_2^q| - 0.92)^2\] (c) Backbone MM-site bond distance [POSITION gradient] (w=3): \[\mathcal{L}_\text{back} = \frac{1}{N_b}\sum_\text{bonds}(d_\text{MM} - 0.7403)^2\] \[d_\text{MM} = \|\mathbf{r}_{i+1} - 0.34\,a_1^{i+1} + 0.3408\,a_2^{i+1} - (\mathbf{r}_i - 0.34\,a_1^i + 0.3408\,a_2^i)\|\]
Why term (c) matters: Pure orientation constraints (a₁⊥a₃, a₂ parallel) have gradient only into ori, not pos. The backbone MM-site depends on both pos and a₁, a₂, so it provides position gradient proportional to how far the backbone bond is from equilibrium — coupling groove shape to chain geometry.
groove_geometry_loss(): geom_loss.py v0.12; added to bridge pos/ori gradient gap; _A1_BACK=−0.34, _A2_BACK=+0.3408 from DNANucleotide.cpp; thermal mean 0.7403 su.

Method

Flow matching: learning to transport noise to structure

Flow matching learns a vector field \(v_t(\mathbf{x})\) that, when integrated from \(t=0\) to \(t=1\), transforms a noisy initial configuration \(\mathbf{x}_0\) (MD start state) into a relaxed target configuration \(\mathbf{x}_1\) (oxDNA2 equilibrium).

Linear interpolation (training target): \[\mathbf{x}_t = (1-t)\,\mathbf{x}_0 + t\,\mathbf{x}_1\] Velocity target: \[u_t(\mathbf{x}_t) = \mathbf{x}_1 - \mathbf{x}_0\] Training objective: \[\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{t,\mathbf{x}_0,\mathbf{x}_1}\left[\|v_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) - u_t(\mathbf{x}_t)\|^2\right]\]
t-bias trick: \(t \sim \text{Uniform}(0,1)^2\) (power=2) biases sampling toward early times \(t \approx 0\). This puts more training signal on the hard initial denoising regime where errors are largest. \[\mathbb{E}[t] = \tfrac{1}{3} \text{ vs } \tfrac{1}{2} \text{ (uniform)}\]
Flow matching: Lipman et al. ICLR 2023; Albergo & Vanden-Eijnden 2022; t-bias for protein flow: Yim et al. 2023.

Theory · Generative Models

From SDE diffusion to ODE flow matching: same marginals, cleaner paths

Diffusion models and flow matching are both continuous-time generative models that learn to transport a noise distribution p₀ to a data distribution p₁. They differ in the stochastic process used and the training objective.

Diffusion (DDPM / Score Matching): \[\text{Forward SDE:}\quad dx = f(x,t)\,dt + g(t)\,dW_t\] \[p_{t|0}(x|x_0) = \mathcal{N}\!\left(x;\,\alpha_t x_0,\,\sigma_t^2 I\right)\] \[\text{Reverse SDE (denoising):}\quad dx = \left[f - g^2\nabla_x\!\log p_t\right]dt + g\,d\bar{W}_t\] The score \(\nabla_x\!\log p_t\) is learned by minimising \(\mathbb{E}[\|\hat{s}_\theta(x_t,t) - \nabla_x\!\log p_{t|0}\|^2]\). Sampling requires hundreds of reverse SDE steps because Brownian noise is added and removed — the path is stochastic.
Probability Flow ODE (Song et al. 2021): \[\frac{dx}{dt} = f(x,t) - \tfrac{1}{2}g(t)^2\,\nabla_x\!\log p_t(x)\] Every diffusion SDE has an equivalent deterministic ODE that produces the same marginal distributions \(p_t\) at every t. Sampling becomes an ODE solve — fewer function evaluations, invertible, no stochastic noise.
Flow Matching (Lipman et al. 2022): \[\frac{dx}{dt} = v_\theta(x_t, t), \quad \mathcal{L}_\text{FM} = \mathbb{E}_{t,x_0,x_1}\!\left[\|v_\theta(x_t,t) - u_t(x_t)\|^2\right]\] \[x_t = (1-t)x_0 + t x_1, \quad u_t = x_1 - x_0 \quad\text{[conditional target velocity]}\] Key insight: instead of learning the score \(\nabla\log p_t\) (which requires marginalising over all data), learn the conditional velocity \(u_t(x_t|x_0,x_1) = x_1 - x_0\) — a simple constant. The marginal flow velocity \(v_t\) is automatically recovered as the mean of conditional velocities under the posterior.
Why flow matching for DNA? DNA origami has SE(3)⁶ᴺ geometry (positions + orientations). SDE diffusion on SO(3) is complex (Brownian motion on Lie groups). Flow matching on SO(3) uses geodesic interpolation R_t = R₀·exp(t·log(R₀ᵀR₁)) — a straight line on the manifold. No stochastic differential equations needed on curved space.
Flow matching: Lipman et al. 2022 (arXiv:2210.02747); Probability Flow ODE: Song et al. 2021 (ICLR 2021); CFM equivalence: Tong et al. 2023; SE(3) flow: Yim et al. 2023 (FrameDiff).

Theory · Architecture

SE(3)-equivariant attention via Clifford geometric algebra: why not just use standard transformers

Standard transformers are not equivariant to rigid-body transformations. If you rotate the DNA structure, the model must re-learn that the same geometry just happened to appear at a different orientation. Equivariance builds this in: rotating the input rotates the output by the same amount.

Equivariance definition: \[f(g \cdot x) = g \cdot f(x) \quad \forall g \in \text{SE}(3)\] where \(g = (R, \mathbf{t})\) is a rigid body transformation (rotation + translation). The model \(f_\theta\) satisfies this exactly — not approximately — regardless of how the input structure is oriented.
Clifford (geometric) algebra approach: \[\text{Clifford algebra } \mathcal{G}(\mathbb{R}^{3,0,1}) \text{ encodes vectors, bivectors, rotors}\] A rotor \(R \in \mathcal{G}\) encodes a rotation: applying it to a vector \(\mathbf{v}\): \[\mathbf{v}' = R\,\mathbf{v}\,\tilde{R} \quad\text{(sandwich product)}\] This is more efficient than 4×4 matrix multiplication. The key property: rotor multiplication is closed — composing two SE(3) rotors gives another SE(3) rotor, preserving equivariance through all attention layers.
Equivariant attention (Clifford attention): \[e_{ij} = \phi_e(\mathbf{h}_i, \mathbf{h}_j, \|\mathbf{r}_{ij}\|^2, \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij})\] \[\mathbf{h}_i' = \sum_j \alpha_{ij}\,\phi_v(\mathbf{h}_j, \hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij})\] Edge features include the invariant \(\|\mathbf{r}_{ij}\|^2\) (distance squared, SE(3)-invariant) and the equivariant unit vector \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}_{ij}\) (transforms as a vector under rotation). Attention weights \(\alpha_{ij}\) are invariant scalars; value aggregation propagates equivariant geometric information.
Why not IPA (Invariant Point Attention from AlphaFold2)? IPA achieves equivariance through explicit frame-relative coordinates — heavy engineering with many special cases. Clifford attention achieves the same equivariance more cleanly through algebraic structure. No explicit frame decomposition needed: the geometry flows naturally through rotor algebra.

For DNA: each nucleotide defines a local frame (a₁, a₂, a₃). The model processes all N nucleotides' frames jointly, updating both positions and orientations in an SE(3)-equivariant way. The same DNA sequence folded at two different global orientations gives predictions that differ only by that same global rotation.

Clifford geometric algebra attention: Brehmer et al. 2023 (arXiv:2302.06594); SE(3) equivariance for molecular generation: Hoogeboom et al. 2022; IPA comparison: Jumper et al. Nature 2021 (AlphaFold2).

Physics

oxDNA2 interaction sites and energy landscape

Each nucleotide in oxDNA2 has three named interaction sites derived from its centre-of-mass position r and orientation vectors a₁, a₃. These sites enter all energy terms.

Base / H-bond site: \[\mathbf{r}_\text{base} = \mathbf{r} + 0.4\,\mathbf{a}_1\] Backbone MM site: \[\mathbf{r}_\text{back} = \mathbf{r} - 0.34\,\mathbf{a}_1 + 0.3408\,\mathbf{a}_3\] Stacking site: COM position \(\mathbf{r}\), angular via \(\mathbf{a}_3\)

The 7 interaction types in the full oxDNA2 energy:

  1. FENE — backbone bond (bonded neighbours)
  2. EXC bonded — excluded volume, bonded pairs
  3. EXC nonbonded — excluded volume, all pairs
  4. Stacking — base stacking (bonded, seq-dependent)
  5. Hydrogen bonding — Watson-Crick HB (non-bonded)
  6. Cross-stacking — across-strand stacking
  7. Debye-Hückel — backbone electrostatics
oxDNA2 constants: Snodin et al. JCP 2015; model.h C++ source; POS_BASE=0.4, POS_MM_BACK1=−0.34, POS_MM_BACK2=0.3408.

Model

ConstrainedDNAFlowModel: 9-step pipeline from input to velocity field

The model takes a noisy configuration at time t and predicts the velocity field (Δpos, ΔR) needed to move toward the target structure. Nine sequential components handle embedding, attention, physics constraints, and SO(3) output.

  1. Input: positions (N,3), orientations (N,3,3), bases (N), time t, mask (N)
  2. Base embedding: {A,C,G,T} → embed_dim via nn.Embedding
  3. Strand embedding: strand_id → strand_embed (max_strands=256)
  4. Position encoding: linear projection of [pos, sin/cos Fourier features]
  5. Time embedding: sinusoidal → MLP → broadcast to all nucleotides
  6. DNAFlowModel backbone: L × CliffordAttentionBlock
  7. BondConstrainedLayer: tanh soft-clamp → 0.5404 su
  8. Output head: predict velocity fields (Δpos, ΔR)
  9. SO(3) output: Gram-Schmidt re-orthonormalization (eps=5e-3)
Critical: eps=5e-3 in Gram-Schmidt. With eps=1e-8, Jacobian=10,000 → peak gradient 6.24M >> bf16 max 65,504 → NaN. Fixed to eps=5e-3: Jacobian=14, peak gradient 8,736.
ConstrainedDNAFlowModel in constrained_flow.py; Gram-Schmidt eps fix: commit 643651c; BondConstrainedLayer targets FENE equilibrium 0.5404 su.

Model Internals

Clifford attention: E3-equivariant geometric algebra on (pos, ori) pairs

Each CliffordAttentionBlock operates on node features augmented with geometric information from positions and orientation matrices. The attention mechanism is equivariant to SE(3) transformations — rotating the entire DNA structure produces consistently rotated outputs.

Edge features (per pair i,j): \[e_{ij} = [d_{ij},\;\hat{r}_{ij},\;{\mathbf{a}_1^i\cdot\mathbf{a}_1^j},\;{\mathbf{a}_3^i\cdot\mathbf{a}_3^j},\;\ldots] \in \mathbb{R}^{e}\] Attention weight: \[\alpha_{ij} = \text{softmax}_j\!\left(\frac{\mathbf{q}_i \cdot \mathbf{k}_j + w(e_{ij})}{\sqrt{d}}\right)\]

Geometric algebra aspect: queries and keys are computed in the Clifford algebra \(\text{Cl}_{3,0}\), which natively encodes rotations as bivectors. This allows the attention score to depend on the relative orientation between nucleotides in an equivariant way.

Attention mask floor: −1×10⁴ (not −∞) for bf16 compatibility. Using −∞ causes NaN in bf16 softmax because −∞ × 0 = NaN.
Clifford geometric algebra attention: clifford_attention.py; SE(3) equivariance via geometric product; attention mask floor −1e4 for bf16; edge features: pairwise dist + orientation dots.

Training · Losses

Supervised losses: position, orientation, and bond

The first three loss terms provide direct supervision from the flow-matching target — they compare predicted velocities against the known displacement from \(\mathbf{x}_0\) to \(\mathbf{x}_1\).

Position loss (w=5.0): \[\mathcal{L}_\text{pos} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i \|\Delta\mathbf{r}_i^\theta - (\mathbf{r}_i^1 - \mathbf{r}_i^0)\|^2\] Orientation loss (w=0.5): \[\mathcal{L}_\text{ori} = \mathcal{L}_\text{geodesic} + \mathcal{L}_{a_1\text{cos}} + \mathcal{L}_{a_3\text{cos}} + \mathcal{L}_\text{angular vel MSE}\] Bond length loss (w=15.0): \[\mathcal{L}_\text{bond} = \frac{1}{B}\sum_\text{bonds} |d_{ij} - 0.5404\,\text{su}|\]

History · Origin

The project began as a protein backbone flow model — then pivoted to DNA in May 2026

The repository's first commit (29e5b9d, May 8 2026) was BBFlow — an SE(3) flow-matching model for protein backbone conformational sampling, built on GAFL and FrameFlow. DNA origami work began with the May 13 commit adding the oxDNA C++ submodule. The BBFlow code was the template for the entire fastDNA architecture.

What transferred from BBFlow to fastDNA:
• SE(3) equivariant attention (Clifford geometric algebra) — kept as-is
• Linear flow matching x_t = (1-t)x₀ + tx₁ — kept
• PyTorch Lightning training loop — kept and extended
• DeepSpeed / gradient checkpointing — kept
• Key difference: protein has 1 backbone atom; DNA has 2 sites (backbone MM + base) with full SO(3) orientation R ∈ SO(3) per nucleotide — 12 DoF vs ~3 DoF per residue
v0.1 (May–Jun 2026): First DNA-specific prototype
Loss = MSE(pred_pos, tgt_pos) + 0.5·MSE(pred_ori, tgt_ori)
Single-strand only. No physics. No geometry constraints. No energy.
v0.2 (Jun 12 2026) — OOM crash: variable-length sequences
Problem: DNA origami has structures from 100 nt to 10,000 nt. Batching mixed lengths in a fixed-size tensor wasted 70–90% of GPU memory on padding and caused CUDA OOM on any batch containing a long structure.
Fix: BucketedSampler groups sequences by similar length into buckets, then pads only within a bucket. 5–12× training speedup; enables batch_size > 1.
BBFlow origin: commit 29e5b9d (2026-05-08); DNA pivot: 9c8366c (2026-05-13); Lightning migration: b800ea0 (2026-06-10); BucketedSampler: e3c5355 (2026-06-12).

History · v0.3–v0.4

First energy attempt blew up; EXC loss was sampling the wrong pairs 99.6% of the time

v0.3 (Jun 10–20): Energy integration crash
Added differentiable oxDNA2 energy to the loss. First run: gradient exploded to NaN within 2 epochs.
Root cause: LJ repulsion V ∝ 1/r¹² has gradient ∝ 1/r¹³. When two nucleotides are at r=0.002 su (random init), the gradient is ~10¹⁴ — instantly overflows bf16 (max 65,504).
Fix: Energy gate — only activate energy loss when bond_mean > 0.45 su AND epoch > 30. Before that, energy is computed for monitoring but its gradient is zeroed.
v0.3: CXSTCK sign error
Cross-stacking energy (CXSTCK) had the wrong formula: f4(θ) + f4(2π-θ) instead of the correct oxDNA2 formula f4(θ) + SA·(θ-SB)².
Validated against oxpy C++ reference (float32 RMSE = 4.7×10⁻⁶). Fixed in v0.4 (oxdna2_pytorch.py).
v0.4 (Jun 21): EXC loss completely blind to local clashes
Added exc_loss to prevent nucleotides overlapping. But used global random sampling — draw K=500 pairs uniformly from all N(N-1)/2 pairs.
For N=5000: local pairs (|i-j|≤50) are 50/5000 = 1% of all pairs.
→ EXC loss hit local (clash-prone) pairs only 0.4% of the time.
→ Inference output had 1000+ steric clashes; EXC loss contribution was 0.015 vs pos_loss 428 — a ratio of 14,000×.

v0.4 also added hb_geometry_loss and stck_ori_loss — first versions with incorrect targets that were later corrected in v0.6–v0.8.

Energy gating: 33845fa, 96259fb; CXSTCK fix: 9f24d8c; EXC local sampling: 1e9438d; v0.3 Lightning migration: b800ea0; full oxDNA2 dataset (9.7M pairs): 96926fb.

History · v0.5–v0.6

MSE orientation loss was dead at θ=π; stacking loss was missing its key constraint entirely

v0.5 (Jun 21): Orientation MSE → geodesic
Problem: MSE loss on 3×3 rotation matrices has gradient = 4·sin(θ). At θ = π (completely wrong — bases pointing the other way), sin(π) = 0 → zero gradient. At t=0 inference, model receives random noise orientations, so all pairs start near θ=π. The model was stuck from step 1.
Fix: Geodesic SO(3) distance arccos((trace(RRᵀ)-1)/2) has constant gradient magnitude = 1, regardless of error size. Added direct a₁·a₁ and a₃·a₃ penalties for the two physically critical axes (H-bond direction and stacking axis).
v0.6 (Jun 23): The flat-sheet collapse — missing helical twist
Inference output looked like a flat 2D sheet, not a 3D helix. Root cause: the stacking geometry loss was missing the helical twist φ constraint entirely.
φ (dihedral angle between consecutive a₂ vectors around the backbone) is the core B-DNA shape constraint — it encodes the 36°/base rotation that makes the double helix. Without it, the model correctly aligned a₃ vectors but had no force that creates the twist → flat sheet.
Fix: Added |cos φ| → 0.656 term (weight ×5), using a₂ (not a₃) as backbone reference.
v0.6: stacking target from wrong data
Old target a₃·a₃ = 0.809 (cos 36°) was measured from DNA origami trajectory data that mixed 5'→3' and 3'→5' strand storage — neighboring nucleotides across a strand break had opposite a₃ directions, so the average was wrong. Correct target 0.921 from BOILERPLATE single-strand equilibration. Also: used COM positions instead of stack sites (pos + 0.34·a₁).
MSE dead zone fix: fe277ea (2026-06-21); geom_loss rewrite: c07dcb2, f290fda (2026-06-23); EXC local sampling: 1e9438d; all equilibria from BOILERPLATE dsDNA MD simulation.

History · v0.7–v0.9

Backbone bonds stuck at 0.39 su for 75 epochs; 94% of dataset was silently discarded

v0.8 (Jun 23): Verified oxDNA2 HB cutoff (was approximate)
Old code used HB_RCUT = 1.5 su (approximate). Verified from DNA2Interaction.cpp: correct value is 2 × POS_BASE (0.4) + HYDR_RCHIGH (0.783775) = 1.5838 su. Using wrong cutoff caused hb_geo loss to fire on non-WC pairs and miss genuine WC pairs.
v0.9 (Jun 24): Bond dead-zone — MSE gradient too weak in compressed range
By epoch 30, bond_mean stabilized at 0.39–0.41 su and refused to move for 75+ epochs. The energy gate required bond_mean > 0.45 — so energy never activated.
Root cause: MSE loss on bond length has gradient = 2·(r−R₀). At r=0.39 su, R₀=0.54 su → gradient = −0.30. This competes with geometry losses that don't care about bond length. The model found a local minimum with compressed bonds.
Fix: FENE bond loss V = −(ε/2)·log(1−((r−R₀)/Δ)²). The logarithm diverges as r → [0.5064, 1.0064] su (the FENE walls), creating very strong gradient near the walls — much stronger than MSE in the compressed region. Also added FORCE_ENERGY=true to bypass the gate entirely.
v0.9: 94% of dataset silently discarded
Any zarr file with N > max_seq_length was dropped entirely. For 5k windows: 3192 of 3381 files were too large → only 189 files used → 1811 training pairs from 129 structures.
Fix: scaffold-aware windowed slicing. Instead of discarding, split large structures into overlapping windows at strand boundaries. 189 files → 3381 files, 1811 → 6300 pairs — 55× more structural diversity.
FENE bond loss: f67f52c; HB cutoff: fe8efcc (1.5838su verified from DNA2Interaction.cpp); dataset fix: 3680b1f; RAM preload: same commit; proportional scheduler: ac77958.

History · v0.10–v0.11

The model was actively learning to collapse all strands into a single blob — for 9 versions

This was the most damaging bug across v0.1–v0.9. The training loss decreased, W&B metrics improved, but every inference output was a dense undifferentiated cluster of nucleotides with no strand structure.

Root cause: The zarr dataset stores nucleotides in sequential order across all strands (nt 0, 1, 2, …, N). Consecutive indices across a strand break have inter-strand distances of 5–35 su. The bond loss treated these as backbone bonds — FENE or MSE tried to pull them to R₀ = 0.54 su.

The model found the optimal solution: minimize inter-strand distances by collapsing everything into one blob. All "bond" losses dropped — but the structure was completely wrong. Visualized in oxView: all nucleotides overlapping at the centroid.
Fix (v0.10, commit a0ce1d3):
strand_mask[i] = consec_dist[i] < 2.0 su — computed from consecutive distances in target positions. True = within strand (backbone bond), False = strand break (ignore).
Applied to: FENE loss, bond_loss, bond_violations metric, bond_mean gate for energy activation.
Also fixed topology export (write_oxdna_top): now correctly writes multi-strand topology instead of one continuous chain — enabling correct visualization in oxView.
v0.11 (Jun 24): Strand ID embedding
Without strand IDs, the transformer attention can't distinguish "same-strand" from "different-strand" neighbors — there's no signal that nucleotides 1823/1824 are covalently bonded while 1824/1825 are on different strands.
Fix: strand_embedding = nn.Embedding(256, 16). Model input expanded 76→92 dims. Same-strand embedding → covalent; different embedding → H-bond / EXC only.
Strand collapse fix: a0ce1d3 (strand_mask, pos weight, hb_geo max); strand_id embedding: f540de2; write_oxdna_top multi-strand: 046797b; strand_mask threshold 2.0su from gap distribution.

Training · Optimization

Reactive weight scheduler: proportional gradient-budget control every 5 epochs

The weight scheduler monitors 9 loss metrics and adjusts weights proportionally — not with fixed steps — to prevent any single term from consuming disproportionate gradient budget.

Update rule (every 5 epochs): \[\Delta w = \text{step} \times \frac{\Delta\mathcal{L} / \mathcal{L}_\text{prev}}{0.05},\quad |\Delta w| \le \text{max\_jump}\]

If loss improves ≥5%: decrease weight. If loss worsens ≥5%: increase weight. Changes are proportional to the magnitude of change — a 10% improvement gives 2× the step of a 5% improvement. Changes less than 5% are damped (scale < 1).

Physics · Repulsion & Electrostatics

Soft-core excluded volume + Debye-Hückel: preventing clashes and enforcing charge screening

Soft-core excluded volume (4 site-pair types): \[V_\text{exc}(r) = \frac{\varepsilon}{(r^2 + \delta^2)^3}, \quad \delta=0.20\text{ su}, \varepsilon=0.01\]

Applied to: backbone–backbone (r_cut=0.89), backbone–base (r_cut=0.81), base–backbone (r_cut=0.81), base–base (r_cut=0.73).
Skip bonded neighbors (|i-j|≤1 same strand).

Debye-Hückel electrostatics (backbone charges): \[V_\text{DH}(r) = \frac{q^2 e^{-r/\lambda}}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r r}, \quad \lambda = 0.3616\text{ su (0.5M NaCl)}\] \[\mathcal{L}_\text{DH} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_\text{pairs} (V_\text{DH}(r) - V_\text{DH}(r_\text{equil}))^2\] Smooth cosine cutoff at r_cut=3.0 su. bf16 clamp: max 50.0 before squaring (prevents Inf at very small r).
Why soft-core, not hard-core? Hard-core exclusion (relu-based) has zero gradient when r ≫ cutoff, so nucleotides that start far apart receive no guidance. Soft-core V ∝ 1/r⁶ provides repulsive gradient at all distances — stronger as nucleotides approach, but non-zero even at large r. This is especially important at early training when structures are random.

DH justification: DNA is highly charged (one phosphate per nt, net −1e per bp). Without DH, nucleotides can be placed at arbitrary proximity along the backbone. At 0.5M NaCl, λ≈1.2 Å ≈ 0.36 su — very short screening length, so only nearest neighbors matter energetically.

exc_geometry_loss() and dh_geometry_loss(): geom_loss.py; EXC δ=0.2, ε=0.01; DH λ=0.3616su, strength=0.0543su; DH_K=300, DH_SALT=0.5M; bf16 clamp at V=50.

Roadmap

Future scope: 6 directions toward full origami inference and beyond

1

Full origami inference (6–12 months): Sliding window approach over 24k-nt scaffold — run model on overlapping 5k-nt windows with strand-boundary alignment, then stitch overlapping predictions using shared nucleotide positions as anchors.

2

Multi-window training (3–6 months): Currently only richest window per large structure. Extend to 2–3 windows per file: the second-richest window exposes different scaffold regions and staple patterns, improving coverage at the same epoch cost.

3

Inverse design (12–24 months): Condition on target 3D shape, generate the scaffold/staple sequence. This reverses the problem: geometry → sequence, enabling rational DNA origami design.

4

Cross-validate vs MD (3 months): Compute RMSD and HB contact maps between fastDNA predictions and ground-truth oxDNA2 MD trajectories on held-out structures. Needed for rigorous benchmarking.

5

Extend to RNA (6–12 months): Same architecture, different force field (oxRNA2). Minimal code changes needed — mainly update energy constants and loss targets.

6

Model size comparison (ongoing): Compare small vs medium vs large at epoch 300+ once medium model has converged. Hypothesis: medium captures more complex scaffold-staple interactions at the cost of slower training.
