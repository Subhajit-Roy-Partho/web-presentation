Prerequisite 1

A protein structure is not a single frozen object

Experimental structures and folding models often give one representative conformation. But in solution, a protein fluctuates among many nearby states. The biologically relevant object is often an ensemble: a distribution over conformations.

Plain-language version: a protein is less like a rigid sculpture and more like a spring-loaded machine whose useful behavior comes from allowed motions.
  • Flexible loops can expose or hide binding sites.
  • Domains can move together or against each other.
  • Mutations can shift population weights between conformations.
Protein dynamics motivation: Adcock and McCammon, Chem. Rev. 2006; BBFlow introduction, Wolf et al. 2025.

Background

The protein backbone: what BBFlow actually models

A protein is a polymer of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. The backbone — the repeating N–Cα–C=O chain — is identical in all amino acids. Only the side chains branching from Cα differ.

BBFlow operates exclusively on backbone frames — one per residue, centred at Cα. Side chains can be added post-hoc with rotamer tools such as Rosetta or DLPacker.
  • Backbone: ~4 heavy atoms per residue; encodes large-scale fold and motion.
  • Side chains: 0–8 more atoms; determine chemistry but not the gross fold.
  • Domain swings, loop openings, and hinge bending are all visible at backbone level.
Backbone frame representation: Jumper et al., AlphaFold, Nature 2021; FrameFlow, Yim et al.; BBFlow, Wolf et al. 2025.

Prerequisite 2

Ensembles come from an energy landscape

At thermal equilibrium, lower-energy conformations are more probable, but higher-energy states can still occur. A simplified Boltzmann view is:

\[ p(x)\propto \exp\left(-\frac{E(x)}{k_BT}\right) \]
  • \(x\): a molecular conformation.
  • \(E(x)\): energy of that conformation.
  • \(k_B T\): thermal energy scale.
  • The ensemble is not only "what structures exist" but "how likely they are."
Boltzmann distribution intuition; see molecular simulation background in Adcock and McCammon, Chem. Rev. 2006.

Prerequisite 3

Molecular dynamics samples motion by simulation

MD starts from an initial structure, assigns velocities, computes forces from a molecular force field, and integrates small time steps. Repeating this produces a trajectory.

\[ m_i\frac{d^2 r_i}{dt^2}=F_i=-\nabla_{r_i}E(r) \]

The problem is scale. Time steps are femtoseconds, but important motions can require nanoseconds to seconds. BBFlow focuses on emulating short standardized MD ensembles, not replacing all physics.

MD overview: Adcock and McCammon, Chem. Rev. 2006; BBFlow scope discussion, Wolf et al. 2025.

Motivation

How conformational dynamics drive biology

Structural flexibility is not noise to be eliminated — it is the mechanism by which proteins perform work. Three phenomena that depend directly on the ensemble:

Allostery

Binding at site A reshapes a distant site B. Many regulatory proteins switch between active and inactive shapes this way.

Induced fit

A drug or substrate reshapes the binding pocket as it docks. Rigid-structure models miss this binding-mode shift.

Cryptic pockets

Drug-binding sites absent in the static fold but transiently open in the ensemble. Ensemble sampling can reveal them.

In drug discovery: a large fraction of clinically validated targets undergo conformational changes critical for inhibitor binding.
Conformational dynamics: Boehr, Nussinov, Wright, Nature Chem. Biol. 2009; cryptic pockets: Oleinikovas et al. 2016.

Prerequisite 4

What does an MD emulator try to learn?

An emulator does not integrate physical forces step by step. It learns a sampling rule that produces structures with similar ensemble statistics to MD under a defined setup.

MD

Physics-based trajectory, expensive, interpretable time evolution.

BBFlow

Learned sampler, fast, targeted at MD-like backbone ensemble statistics.

That distinction matters: BBFlow is evaluated as an MD emulator, not as a universal predictor of all rare conformational states.

MD-emulation framing and standardized evaluation: Wolf et al. 2025; ATLAS, Vander Meersche et al., NAR 2024.

Prerequisite 5

Transformer attention in one slide

Attention lets each token decide which other tokens to read from. In proteins, a token might be a residue, an MSA position, or a learned structural node.

\[ \mathrm{Attention}(Q,K,V)= \mathrm{softmax}\left(\frac{QK^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V \]
  • Query: what this position needs.
  • Key: what another position advertises.
  • Value: information transferred when the match is strong.
Vaswani et al., Attention Is All You Need, NeurIPS 2017.

Prerequisite 6

Proteins need more than sequence attention

Protein models often need to combine residue identity, long-range contacts, and 3D geometry. Two residues far apart in sequence can be close in space and functionally linked.

In AlphaFold-like systems, pair representations and geometric modules help the model reason about contact maps, distances, orientations, and 3D consistency.

BBFlow keeps the geometric part but removes the expensive sequence-evolution machinery.

AlphaFold pair/geometric representation context: Jumper et al., Nature 2021; BBFlow geometric conditioning, Wolf et al. 2025.

Prerequisite 7

Geometry-aware models must respect coordinate symmetry

A protein's stored coordinates depend on an arbitrary lab frame. If we rotate or translate the input, physical conclusions should not change arbitrarily.

\[ f(g\cdot x)=\rho_{\mathrm{out}}(g)f(x) \]
  • Invariant output: unchanged by a global rotation or translation, such as an energy.
  • Equivariant output: rotates/translates consistently, such as predicted coordinates.
  • \(SE(3)\) is the group of 3D rotations and translations.
Geometric deep learning context: Tensor Field Networks; SE(3)-Transformer; EGNN; BBFlow uses SE(3)-equivariant modeling.

Prerequisite 8

BBFlow represents the backbone as residue frames

Instead of predicting only points, many protein backbone models represent each residue by a rigid body frame:

\[ x=(r,z)\in SE(3)^N,\qquad r\in SO(3),\quad z\in\mathbb{R}^3 \]
  • \(z\): residue position, often tied to C-alpha/backbone geometry.
  • \(r\): local orientation of the backbone at that residue.
  • A full backbone is a sequence of \(N\) such frames.
Frame representation: AlphaFold structure module, FrameDiff, FrameFlow, GAFL, and BBFlow.

Prerequisite 9

Invariant Point Attention: attention with 3D geometry

AlphaFold's IPA augments attention with learned 3D points. Residues create query/key/value points in their local frames, compare points in global space, then transform outputs back to local frames.

IPA is "standard attention plus geometry-aware distance terms," designed so attention does not depend on the arbitrary global coordinate frame.
Jumper et al., Highly accurate protein structure prediction with AlphaFold, Nature 2021.

Prerequisite 10

CFA here means Clifford Frame Attention

BBFlow adapts GAFL, where CFA extends IPA using projective geometric algebra. Instead of only learned points, features can represent richer geometric objects and use algebraic products to build messages.

Terminology note: this CFA is not frame averaging. Frame averaging is a separate symmetry-building method.
  • IPA: points in local frames.
  • CFA: multivectors representing points, lines, planes, and frames.
  • Goal: more expressive geometric messages between residues.
Wagner et al., Generating Highly Designable Proteins with Geometric Algebra Flow Matching, NeurIPS 2024; BBFlow method section.

Background — ML

Diffusion models vs. flow matching

Both families learn to transform a simple prior into a complex data distribution. They differ in how they parameterize that transformation.

Diffusion (DDPM/score)

Add Gaussian noise in many incremental steps. Learn to reverse the noising process via a score function. Inference integrates a stochastic SDE.

Typical steps at inference: 100–1000.

Flow matching

Learn a deterministic velocity field that transports prior to data along geodesics. Inference integrates an ODE — straighter paths, fewer evaluations.

BBFlow steps: 20.

Flow matching also allows non-Gaussian priors — a strict constraint for diffusion models. BBFlow exploits this to use a conditional prior that starts near the equilibrium structure.
Diffusion models: Ho et al. DDPM, NeurIPS 2020; flow matching: Lipman et al. ICLR 2023; Riemannian FM: Chen and Lipman, ICLR 2024.

Prerequisite 11

Flow matching learns a transport field

The model learns a time-dependent vector field that moves simple prior samples into data samples. During generation, we integrate the learned ordinary differential equation.

\[ \frac{d}{dt}\phi_t(x)=v(\phi_t(x),t),\qquad \phi_0(x)=x \]

For intuition, imagine a learned wind field that pushes random particles into the shape of the data distribution.

Lipman et al., Flow Matching for Generative Modeling, ICLR 2023.

Prerequisite 12

For proteins, the flow lives on SE(3)^N

Because each residue is a frame, interpolation is not just a straight line in Cartesian space. Rotations move along SO(3) geodesics and translations move through R^3.

\[ v_{SO(3)}(r_t,t\mid r_1)=\frac{\log_{r_t}(r_1)}{1-t}, \quad v_{\mathbb{R}^3}(z_t,t\mid z_1)=\frac{z_1-z_t}{1-t} \]

The log map is the rotation-space analogue of "direction from current orientation to target orientation."

FrameFlow and SE(3) protein flow matching: Yim et al., arXiv:2310.05297; BBFlow background equations.

Literature buildup

Earlier work: three routes toward protein ensembles

Physics-first

MD and enhanced sampling integrate physical dynamics. Strong mechanistic grounding, but expensive for broad screening.

Boltzmann Generators learn equilibrium sampling with flows, often system-specific.

Folding-model-based

AlphaFlow / ESMFlow repurpose AlphaFold or ESMFold under flow matching.

Powerful, but tied to large pre-trained models and evolutionary information.

Geometric generative models

FrameDiff, FrameFlow, FoldFlow, GAFL generate protein backbones over \(SE(3)^N\).

BBFlow imports this machinery into MD-emulating ensemble generation.

Noe et al., Science 2019; Jing et al., AlphaFlow, ICML 2024; Yim et al., FrameDiff/FrameFlow; Wagner et al., GAFL.

Why BBFlow?

The gap: ensemble generation without solving folding again

AlphaFlow-style models can generate ensembles, but they are built around structure-prediction machinery. That machinery is expensive and depends on sequence/evolutionary information.

BBFlow asks: if we already have the equilibrium structure, can geometry alone be enough to sample MD-like local conformations?
  • Useful for MD replacement, because MD also starts from a structure.
  • Useful for de novo proteins, where MSAs may be weak or absent.
  • Useful for speed, because no Evoformer or repeated folding trunk is needed.
Problem framing and motivation: Wolf et al., arXiv:2503.05738v2; AlphaFlow baseline: Jing et al., ICML 2024.

BBFlow core

The central modeling move

BBFlow treats an ensemble as a conditional distribution over backbone conformations:

\[ p(x\mid x_{\mathrm{eq}}) \]

Here \(x_{\mathrm{eq}}\) is the input equilibrium backbone structure and \(x\) is a sampled conformation from the MD-like ensemble.

The model is not asked to infer the fold from sequence. It is asked to generate likely fluctuations around a known fold.
Conditional ensemble formulation: Wolf et al., Section 3.

BBFlow method

Encoding the equilibrium structure

BBFlow replaces evolutionary contact information with direct geometry from the equilibrium backbone.

\[ s_{ij}=\mathrm{bin}(\|z_i-z_j\|_2) \] \[ e_{ij}=r_i^{-1}\left(\frac{z_i-z_j}{\|z_i-z_j\|_2}\right) \]
  • Distance bins: encode which residues are near or far in the fold.
  • Local directions: encode where residue \(j\) sits in residue \(i\)'s local frame.
  • Amino-acid identity: optional local chemistry signal used as a node feature.
Equilibrium distance and direction encodings: Wolf et al., Section 3, Eqs. 6-7.

BBFlow method

The conditional prior starts near the protein fold

Standard protein flow models often begin from generic noise. BBFlow first samples generic \(SE(3)^N\) noise, then moves it partway toward the equilibrium structure along a geodesic.

\[ x_{\mathrm{uncond}}\sim p_{\mathrm{uncond}},\qquad x_0=\gamma(x_{\mathrm{uncond}},x_{\mathrm{eq}},\xi) \]

The main model uses \(\xi=0.2\). Smaller \(\xi\) means noisier and more diverse starts; larger \(\xi\) means more constrained starts.

Conditional prior: Wolf et al., Section 3, Eqs. 8-9; recreated from Fig. A.4.

BBFlow method

Architecture: shallow geometric network, not a folding trunk

BBFlow adapts GAFL. It receives current frames \(x_t\), time \(t\), pairwise features, amino-acid identities, and equilibrium geometry. It predicts target frames \(\hat{x}_1\), which define the predicted vector field.

  • SE(3)-equivariant graph neural network.
  • Six CFA message-passing blocks.
  • No Evoformer, no MSA search, no pre-trained folding model.
  • No residue-index feature, helping transfer to multi-chain systems.
Architecture details: Wolf et al., Section 3; GAFL/CFA: Wagner et al., NeurIPS 2024.

BBFlow method

Training objective: regress the geodesic velocity

For each training example, BBFlow knows a target MD conformation \(x_1\). It samples \(x_0\) from the conditional prior, picks a time \(t\), and constructs an intermediate \(x_t\) along the geodesic.

\[ x_t=\gamma(x_0,x_1,t) \] \[ \mathcal{L}_{FM}= \mathbb{E}\left[\|v-\hat{v}(x_t,t,x_{\mathrm{eq}})\|^2_{SE(3)}\right] \]

The network is trained to predict the velocity that would continue from \(x_t\) toward \(x_1\).

BBFlow loss: Wolf et al., Section 3, Eqs. 10-12; FrameFlow/FrameDiff auxiliary loss background.

BBFlow training

Training algorithm in words

1

Sample a protein equilibrium structure \(x_{\mathrm{eq}}\) and one MD conformation \(x_1\).

2

Sample \(x_0\) from the conditional prior \(p_0(\cdot\mid x_{\mathrm{eq}})\).

3

Sample time \(t\sim U(0,1)\) and interpolate \(x_t=\gamma(x_0,x_1,t)\).

4

Compute the ground-truth tangent vector \(v\) from \(x_t\) toward \(x_1\).

5

Run the network to predict \(\hat{x}_1\), convert it into \(\hat{v}\), and minimize the SE(3) flow-matching loss.

Algorithm 1 and Section 3 in Wolf et al., arXiv:2503.05738v2.

Metrics — visual

RMSF in pictures: what we are measuring

RMSF (Root-Mean-Square Fluctuation) per residue measures how much each Cα atom moves across the ensemble after structural alignment.

\[ \mathrm{RMSF}_i = \sqrt{\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\bigl\|r_i(t) - \bar{r}_i\bigr\|^2} \]
  • High RMSF: flexible loops, disordered termini, exposed surface regions.
  • Low RMSF: rigid secondary-structure cores — buried helices and strands.
  • MAE metric: how close are BBFlow's per-residue values to MD's amplitudes?
  • Correlation: do we correctly rank flexible vs. rigid residues?

A good model gets both: the pattern of flexibility (correlation) and the magnitude (MAE).

RMSF metric used in AlphaFlow (Jing et al. 2024) and BBFlow (Wolf et al. 2025); ATLAS MD reference (Vander Meersche et al. 2024).

Evaluation setup

What the metrics mean

RMSF

Per-residue flexibility. Correlation asks whether flexible regions are in the right places; MAE asks whether amplitudes match.

Pairwise RMSD

Average structural spread between conformations. Useful for detecting over-stabilized or too-diffuse ensembles.

DCCM

Dynamic cross-correlation matrix. Measures whether residue motions are correlated, anticorrelated, or independent.

PCA \(W_2\)

Projects generated states onto dominant MD motions and compares distributions with Wasserstein-2 distance.

Transient contacts

Rare contacts that are absent in the equilibrium structure but appear in part of the ensemble.

Inference time

Time per generated conformation. Critical if the model is used for large-scale protein screening.

Metric definitions from Wolf et al., Section 4 and Appendix A.3; AlphaFlow metrics from Jing et al. 2024.

Main result

Speed/accuracy tradeoff

The strongest practical claim is not that BBFlow wins every metric. It is that BBFlow lands in a strong region of the Pareto curve: accurate enough for MD-emulation benchmarks while much faster.

  • AlphaFlow-T: strong accuracy, slow, over-stabilized median RMSF.
  • Distilled AlphaFlow variants: faster, but lose ensemble spread.
  • BBFlow: fast and close to MD median RMSF.

Related work

Complementary approaches: BioEmu, AlphaFlow-T, and BBFlow

BBFlow

Target: MD-like short-timescale ensemble (~300 ns trajectory statistics).

Input: equilibrium backbone only — no MSA, no language model, no pre-trained folding weights.

Speed: 0.8 s per conformation; trained from scratch in 3 GPU-days on 2× A100.

Scope: ATLAS proteins, de novo proteins, multi-chain monomers.

BioEmu (Lewis et al. 2025)

Target: general Boltzmann distribution; trained on ~500 ms of enhanced-sampling MD.

Input: sequence + ESM protein language model embeddings.

Speed: ~1.9 s per conformation.

Scope: captures rarer conformational states and longer-timescale transitions beyond ATLAS.

AlphaFlow-T (Jing et al. 2024)

Target: same ATLAS 300 ns MD distribution as BBFlow.

Input: sequence + MSA + equilibrium structure as AlphaFold template.

Speed: 32.6 s per conformation.

Scope: highest accuracy on transient contacts; relies on expensive evolutionary machinery.

Which tool to use: fast MD emulator for many proteins with no MSA (BBFlow) vs. general alternative-state explorer (BioEmu) vs. highest accuracy with evolutionary data (AlphaFlow-T).
BioEmu: Lewis et al., Science 2025; AlphaFlow: Jing et al., ICML 2024; comparison caveats in Wolf et al. 2025, Table 1 footnote.

Generalization

De novo proteins test the evolutionary-information assumption

De novo designed proteins often lack natural homologs. That makes MSA-based or evolutionary-statistics-based ensemble generation harder to trust.

In the paper, AlphaFlow without templates and BioEmu show strong performance degradation on the de novo benchmark, while BBFlow remains close to template-conditioned baselines.

Generalization

Multi-chain transfer comes from geometric conditioning

BBFlow omits absolute residue and chain-index features. Instead, it uses the geometry of the equilibrium structure. That makes the same graph machinery naturally applicable to complexes.

0.31RMSF MAE
0.85DCCM r

The paper reports multi-chain results on five systems, even though BBFlow was trained only on monomers.

Usage

BBFlow in practice: from PDB entry to ensemble

1

Obtain an equilibrium structure. Fetch a PDB entry, run AlphaFold on the sequence, or use any predicted backbone. BBFlow only needs Cα frames — no side chains required as input.

2

Encode the backbone geometry. BBFlow computes pairwise Cα distances (binned) and local pairwise directions for residue pairs within 5 Å. This replaces the MSA pipeline entirely.

3

Sample from the conditional prior. Generic SE(3) noise is geodesically interpolated 20% toward the equilibrium structure, giving a protein-aware starting point for the flow.

4

Integrate the flow and generate conformations. The SE(3) ODE is integrated over 20 steps. Each call produces one backbone conformation. Generate 250+ for a statistically useful ensemble in under 5 minutes on a GPU.

5

Analyse and apply. Compute RMSF profiles, cluster conformers, identify flexible loops, discover cryptic pockets, or supply conformations as receptor inputs for ensemble-aware docking. Code: github.com/graeter-group/bbflow.

Workflow from BBFlow repository and Wolf et al. 2025, Section 4; no MSA, Evoformer, or pre-trained folding weights required at inference.

Mechanistic evidence

Ablations show which design choices mattered

The most important feature is the equilibrium distance encoding. Without it, the model loses the fold-level condition and performance collapses.

  • Conditional prior improves performance and ensemble spread.
  • Direction encoding adds useful local geometry.
  • Removing residue-index encoding is not harmful and supports multi-chain transfer.
  • Distance encoding is essential.
Ablation results from Wolf et al., Table 3 and Appendix Table A.12.

Practical

Training from scratch: an accessible compute budget

BBFlow was trained without any pre-trained folding model weights. Starting from random initialization, the full training run on ATLAS (1265 proteins, 3×100 ns each) requires:

2× A10040 GB NVIDIA GPUs
~144 GPU-htotal compute estimate

AlphaFold fine-tuning route

Requires AlphaFold 2 weights, an MSA pipeline, and Evoformer-level memory. Fine-tuning alone takes significant additional compute on top of the original multi-thousand GPU-hour AlphaFold training.

BBFlow from scratch

No pre-trained starting point required. Any lab with two modern research GPUs can reproduce, fine-tune on a custom force field, or extend the model to new domains within days.

Teaching synthesis

How to explain BBFlow in one minute

1

Proteins are distributions of moving structures, not single static shapes.

2

MD samples those distributions but is too expensive for broad use.

3

Previous ML ensemble models often reuse folding models and evolutionary information.

4

BBFlow says: given the equilibrium backbone, learn the local MD-like distribution directly from geometry.

5

The model moves conditional noise through an SE(3) flow to generate backbone ensemble samples quickly.

Synthesis of Wolf et al., Sections 1-5.

Critical reading

What BBFlow does not solve

  • It emulates short-timescale MD-like distributions; it is not a universal rare-state predictor.
  • It is limited by the MD data distribution, force-field assumptions, and sampling length.
  • It is weaker than MSA/template-aware models on transient contacts.
  • It needs an input equilibrium structure, though MD also needs an initial structure.
  • It generates backbone ensembles, not side-chain ensembles or protein-ligand interaction ensembles.
Limitations summarized from Wolf et al., Section 4.5 and Appendix discussion.

Open questions

What comes next: open questions and future directions

Side-chain ensembles

BBFlow predicts backbone frames only. Extending to side-chain rotamer distributions would enable full-atom ensemble predictions and more accurate drug-interaction modelling.

Longer timescales

ATLAS covers ~300 ns. Microsecond and millisecond events — large domain motions, partial unfolding, rare state transitions — require enhanced-sampling MD training data or new loss functions.

Cryptic pocket discovery

Fast ensemble generation enables high-throughput screening for transiently exposed pockets invisible in static structures — directly feeding fragment-based and structure-based drug design.

Protein–ligand systems

Extending to receptor–ligand complexes and membrane proteins would unlock ensemble-aware docking, where the receptor is flexible rather than treated as rigid.

Custom MD distributions

Training from scratch in days means any lab can retrain BBFlow on specialized force fields, different temperatures, or custom simulation protocols — no AlphaFold-scale infrastructure required.

Beyond monomeric proteins

The geometry-conditioning framework is not protein-specific. RNA conformational ensembles, disordered proteins, and protein–nucleic acid complexes are natural extensions of the same approach.

Future directions from Wolf et al. 2025, Section 4.5 limitations; drug-discovery context: Salo-Ahen et al. 2020.

Conclusion

Key takeaways

Conceptual shift

Decouple ensemble generation from sequence-to-structure prediction by conditioning on an equilibrium backbone.

Technical shift

Use a conditional prior and SE(3) flow matching over residue frames, with geometry replacing MSA-derived contacts.

Practical result

Comparable MD-emulation accuracy at much lower inference time, plus stronger de novo and multi-chain applicability.

BBFlow's central message: for local MD-like ensembles, backbone geometry carries enough information to replace much of the expensive evolutionary/folding-model machinery.
Wolf et al., Learning conformational ensembles of proteins based on backbone geometry, NeurIPS 2025.
